## Notes

- **v2:** The ruler’s claim, “I am a god,” is answered not by denying his power or wealth but by insisting that he is *human*. The repeated contrast exposes the fatal reach of political pride.
- **v3:** “Daniel” may refer to the renowned wise figure known from ancient tradition, rather than necessarily to Ezekiel’s contemporary. The verse is spoken with biting irony: the ruler imagines that no mystery escapes his insight.
- **v12–19:** The lament addresses the king of Tyre while using Eden, cherub, holy-mountain, and precious-stone imagery. The poem does not explain every feature of its imagery; it heightens the king’s former glory in order to make his corruption and downfall more devastating.
- **v13:** Several gemstones cannot be identified with certainty in modern mineral names. The translation preserves the traditional sequence while recognizing that ancient and modern classifications do not always correspond.
- **v14:** The Hebrew shifts grammatical gender in this verse and nearby lines. The shifts may arise from the poem’s changing imagery; the English retains the direct address without attempting to resolve the change.
- **v16:** Trade is not condemned in itself. The indictment is that abundant trade filled Tyre’s midst with violence, joining economic success to exploitation and injustice.
- **v18:** “Your sanctuaries” is plural. In the poem’s imagery, what had been set apart as holy has been profaned by injustice.
- **v22–23:** God’s glory and holiness are made known through judgment on Sidon. In Ezekiel, judgment is repeatedly bound to the recognition formula: “Then they will know that I am the LORD.”
- **v25–26:** The chapter closes with restoration for Israel. Secure dwelling, homes, and vineyards stand in deliberate contrast to the violence, ruin, and dispossession announced for Tyre and Sidon.

## Vocabulary

- **v2: לֵב (*lev*)** — “heart.” In this passage the heart is the center of judgment, desire, and self-understanding. The ruler’s heart makes itself like a god’s heart.
- **v5: רְכֻלָּה (*rekhullah*)** — “trade” or “commerce.” The term recurs in the lament, where commercial abundance becomes the setting for violence and injustice.
- **v7: חָלַל (*chalal*)** — “profane, defile.” The invaders will profane Tyre’s splendor; later the king is cast out as profane and is said to have profaned his sanctuaries.
- **v12: קִינָה (*qinah*)** — “lament.” This is a formal funeral song, though its subject is still living; its poetic praise is shaped from the beginning by the certainty of downfall.
- **v14: כְּרוּב (*keruv*)** — “cherub.” The cherub belongs to sacred, guarded space. Its use intensifies the poem’s portrayal of exalted privilege and subsequent expulsion.
- **v16: חָמָס (*chamas*)** — “violence.” The word denotes wrongdoing that injures and violates others, not merely physical force.
- **v18: עָוֹן (*avon*)** — “iniquity.” Here it names accumulated guilt that has distorted what was entrusted to the king.
- **v22: קָדַשׁ (*qadash*)** — “be holy; show oneself holy.” God’s holiness is manifested publicly through acts of judgment and restoration.
- **v26: בֶּטַח (*betach*)** — “security.” Israel’s promised security is repeated for emphasis and is grounded in God’s judgment on those who despise them.
