# Ecclesiastes 12

v01:
Remember your Creator in the days of your youth,
before the days of trouble come
and the years draw near when you will say,
“I have no delight in them”—

v02:
before the sun and the light grow dark,
and the moon and the stars,
and the clouds return after the rain;

v03:
on the day when the guards of the house tremble,
and the strong men bend themselves,
and the women who grind cease because they are few,
and those who look through the windows grow dim;

v04:
when the doors are shut in the street,
as the sound of the mill grows low;
when one rises at the sound of a bird,
and all the daughters of song are brought low;

v05:
when they are afraid of heights,
and terrors are in the road;
when the almond tree blossoms,
the grasshopper drags itself along,
and the caper berry fails—
for a person goes to their everlasting home,
and the mourners circle in the street;

v06:
before the silver cord is snapped,
and the golden bowl is crushed,
and the jar is broken at the spring,
and the wheel is crushed at the well;

v07:
and the dust returns to the earth as it was,
and the spirit returns to God who gave it.

v08:
“Vapor of vapors,” says the Teacher;
“all is vapor.”

v09:
<p>
Beyond this, because the Teacher was wise, he continually taught the people knowledge. He weighed and searched out and set in order many proverbs.
</p>

v10:
<p>
The Teacher sought to find words of delight, and to write upright words of truth.
</p>

v11:
<p>
The words of the wise are like goads, and like firmly fixed nails are the masters of collections; they are given by one shepherd.
</p>

v12:
<p>
And beyond these, my child, be warned: there is no end to the making of many books, and much study wearies the flesh.
</p>

v13:
<p>
The end of the matter; all has been heard: fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole of humanity.
</p>

v14:
<p>
For God will bring every deed into judgment, with every hidden thing, whether good or evil.
</p>

## Notes

**12:1 — “your Creator.”** The Hebrew form is plural, literally “your Creators,” though it takes a singular sense here. The plural may heighten the majesty and fullness of God as Creator.

**12:3–6 — Images of decline and death.** The poem depicts a darkening house, failing workers, shut doors, and broken vessels. Many readers hear images of an aging body in these lines, but the poem does not assign a single bodily meaning to every image; its accumulating force is the fragility and approaching end of life.

**12:5 — “the caper berry fails.”** The caper berry was associated with stimulation or desire. Its failure may evoke the fading of appetite and vitality, while still belonging to the poem’s larger sequence of fading natural and household images.

**12:7 — “the spirit.”** The Hebrew *ruach* can mean spirit, breath, or wind. The verse holds together bodily mortality—dust returning to earth—and the life given by God returning to God, without explaining the full nature of that return.

**12:8 — “Vapor of vapors.”** This repeated declaration echoes Ecclesiastes 1:2. “Vapor” conveys what is fleeting, elusive, and impossible to grasp or secure; it does not simply mean that all things are worthless.

**12:11 — “masters of collections.”** The phrase may refer to those responsible for assembling and preserving sayings, or to collected sayings themselves. The “one shepherd” can be understood as the ultimate source of wise instruction, though the text does not explicitly identify the shepherd.

**12:13 — “the whole of humanity.”** The Hebrew expression is compact and may mean “the whole duty of humanity,” “what is fitting for every person,” or “the whole of humanity.” The translation preserves its broad claim: reverence for God and obedience to God’s commands concern human life as such.

## Vocabulary

**12:1 — בָּרָא (*bara’*), “create.”** Used here in the plural form “your Creator,” this word directs the reader to remember God not merely as a helper in youth but as the One to whom life itself belongs.

**12:5 — עוֹלָם (*‘olam*), “everlasting.”** In “everlasting home,” the term reaches beyond a long duration to a horizon not bounded by ordinary human time. Death is named as the destination toward which a person goes.

**12:7 — רוּחַ (*ruach*), “spirit, breath, wind.”** The word describes the life that returns to God who gave it. Its range keeps breath and spirit closely joined rather than separating bodily life from God’s sustaining gift.

**12:8 — הֶבֶל (*hevel*), “vapor.”** The book’s defining word names what is fleeting, insubstantial, and resistant to human control. Its threefold repetition intensifies the Teacher’s judgment.

**12:11 — רֹעֶה (*ro‘eh*), “shepherd.”** The image of the “one shepherd” presents wise words as guidance given under a singular, sustaining source.

**12:13 — יָרֵא (*yare’*), “fear.”** To fear God is not mere terror; in this concluding command it names reverent recognition of God’s authority, joined with the keeping of God’s commandments.

**12:14 — מִשְׁפָּט (*mishpat*), “judgment.”** God’s judgment encompasses every deed and every hidden thing. The final word of the book places both visible actions and concealed realities before God.
