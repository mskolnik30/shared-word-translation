## Notes

- **2:1, 11.** “Vapor” renders *hevel*, a recurring word in Ecclesiastes. It evokes breath or mist: something real yet fleeting, elusive, and impossible to secure. The Teacher’s verdict does not deny that pleasure or work can be experienced; it questions their lasting gain.

- **2:3.** The Teacher’s experiment with wine is not presented as simple abandonment to intoxication. He seeks to hold wine, wisdom, and folly together in order to discover what good human beings might find during their brief lives.

- **2:8.** The final expression, rendered “a consort and consorts,” is unusually obscure. Its precise reference is uncertain; it may concern women in a royal household, though other explanations have been proposed. The phrase heightens the catalogue of possessions and pleasures without resolving its exact sense.

- **2:11.** “A chasing after wind” preserves the image of pursuing what cannot be caught or retained. The phrase appears again at key moments in the chapter, joining the Teacher’s observations about achievement, wisdom, and wealth.

- **2:14–16.** Wisdom truly has an advantage over folly, as light over darkness. Yet the shared fate of death and eventual forgetfulness prevents wisdom from becoming an ultimate safeguard. The passage holds these claims together rather than cancelling either one.

- **2:24–26.** After the exhaustion of labor and possession, the Teacher names eating, drinking, and receiving good from one’s work as gifts from God. This is not a return to self-made pleasure: the ability to enjoy is itself received “from the hand of God.”

- **2:25.** The Hebrew reads, “apart from me,” though the immediate reference is difficult. It may reflect the Teacher’s own voice, emphasizing his exceptional experience, while some ancient textual traditions read “apart from him,” referring to God.

## Vocabulary

- **1 — הֶבֶל (*hevel*):** “Vapor” describes what cannot be held or made permanent. In this chapter it is the Teacher’s verdict on pleasure, accomplishment, wisdom’s ultimate limits, and accumulated wealth.

- **10 — חֵלֶק (*cheleq*):** “Portion” is what one receives or enjoys from life and labor. The Teacher initially calls enjoyment of labor his portion, yet later confronts the possibility that this portion will pass to another.

- **11 — רְעוּת רוּחַ (*reʿut ruaḥ*):** “A chasing after wind.” The image portrays striving that expends effort without securing a lasting result.

- **13 — יִתְרוֹן (*yitron*):** “Advantage” or lasting gain. Wisdom has a real advantage over folly, but the Teacher repeatedly asks whether any such advantage endures under the sun.

- **21 — עָמָל (*ʿamal*):** “Labor,” often carrying the burden, weariness, and anxiety of toil. The word gathers the chapter’s concern that what one works for may not remain in one’s own hands.

- **24 — נֶפֶשׁ (*nephesh*):** Rendered “life.” Here it is the living self that is enabled to see or experience good in labor.
