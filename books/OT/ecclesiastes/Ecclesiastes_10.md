v01:
<p>
Dead flies make the perfumer’s oil stink—make it bubble;  
so a little folly outweighs wisdom and honor.
</p>

v02:
<p>
The heart of the wise inclines to the right;  
the heart of the fool, to the left.
</p>

v03:
<p>
Even when the fool walks along the road,  
his heart is lacking,  
and he tells everyone, “He is a fool.”
</p>

v04:
<p>
If the ruler’s anger rises against you,  
do not leave your place,  
for calmness puts great offenses to rest.
</p>

v05:
<p>
There is an evil I have seen under the sun,  
like an error that goes out from the ruler:
</p>

v06:
<p>
Folly is set in many high places,  
while the rich sit in low places.
</p>

v07:
<p>
I have seen servants on horses  
and officials walking on the ground like servants.
</p>

v08:
<p>
Whoever digs a pit will fall into it,  
and whoever breaks through a wall will be bitten by a snake.
</p>

v09:
<p>
Whoever moves stones will be hurt by them;  
whoever splits logs will be endangered by them.
</p>

v10:
<p>
If the iron is dull  
and one does not sharpen its edge,  
one must use greater strength;  
but wisdom has the advantage of bringing success.
</p>

v11:
<p>
If a snake bites before it is charmed,  
there is no advantage for the master of the tongue.
</p>

v12:
<p>
The words from the mouth of the wise are gracious,  
but the lips of a fool consume him.
</p>

v13:
<p>
The beginning of the words of his mouth is folly,  
and the end of his mouth is wicked madness.
</p>

v14:
<p>
The fool multiplies words.  
No one knows what will be;  
and what will be after him—  
who can tell him?
</p>

v15:
<p>
The toil of fools wearies them,  
for they do not know how to go to a city.
</p>

v16:
<p>
Woe to you, O land, when your king is a youth  
and your officials feast in the morning!
</p>

v17:
<p>
Happy are you, O land, when your king is a son of nobles  
and your officials eat at the proper time—  
for strength, and not for drinking.
</p>

v18:
<p>
Through laziness the roof sinks in,  
and through slack hands the house leaks.
</p>

v19:
<p>
They make bread for laughter,  
and wine gladdens life,  
and money answers everything.
</p>

v20:
<p>
Even in your thoughts do not curse a king,  
and in your bedroom do not curse the rich;  
for a bird of the air may carry the sound,  
and a winged creature may tell the matter.
</p>

## Notes

- **v1:** The image is deliberately disproportionate: a few dead flies can ruin costly perfumer’s oil. The final line similarly says that a small amount of folly can carry more weight than wisdom and honor.
- **v4:** “The ruler’s anger” is literally the ruler’s *spirit* rising against someone. “Calmness” may mean composure, gentleness, or a yielding response; it has the power to avert the consequences of serious offenses.
- **v10:** The Hebrew speaks literally of not sharpening the “face” of the iron—that is, its cutting edge. Wisdom does not eliminate effort, but enables effort to be directed effectively.
- **v11:** “Master of the tongue” may refer to a snake-charmer, whose skill is useless if the snake bites before being charmed. The phrase may also carry an echo of the danger and limits of skilled speech.
- **v19:** The saying is terse and potentially ironic. Bread, wine, and money are presented as meeting practical needs or answering demands, but the verse does not claim that wealth finally answers every human question.
- **v20:** The warning uses the vivid possibility that even private words may somehow be carried away and reported. The image counsels caution amid unequal power.

## Vocabulary

- **v1 — סִכְלוּת (*sikhlut*):** “folly.” Here it is not mere lack of intelligence but conduct so ruinous that even a little of it can outweigh wisdom and honor.
- **v2 — לֵב (*lev*):** “heart.” In this passage the heart is the center of judgment, intention, and practical orientation, not merely emotion.
- **v4 — רוּחַ (*ruach*):** “spirit, wind, breath.” The ruler’s *ruach* can denote an aroused temper or disposition directed against another.
- **v10 — יִתְרוֹן (*yitron*):** “advantage, gain.” A recurring term in Ecclesiastes, here describing wisdom’s practical benefit in making action effective.
- **v13 — הוֹלֵלוּת (*holelut*):** “madness, reckless folly.” The fool’s speech progresses from folly into a destructive and morally dangerous excess.
- **v18 — עַצְלָה (*atslah*):** “laziness.” The image of a collapsing roof turns neglected work into a visible picture of deterioration.
