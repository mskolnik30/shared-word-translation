## Notes

**14:2** “That you are”: The Hebrew text preserves both a written form and a traditional reading here. Both yield the same sense: Jeroboam’s wife is to conceal her identity.  

**14:6** “A hard message”: Ahijah’s blindness does not prevent him from recognizing her. The scene contrasts human inability to see with the LORD’s knowledge and prophetic disclosure.  

**14:10** “Everyone who urinates against a wall”: This is a deliberately coarse Hebrew expression for males. Its bluntness intensifies the announced elimination of Jeroboam’s household.  

**14:13** “Something good”: In the midst of judgment on Jeroboam’s house, the child alone receives mourning and burial because the LORD finds good in him. The text does not explain further what that good is.  

**14:14** “And what? Even now!”: The Hebrew is abrupt and elliptical, heightening the immediacy of the announced judgment. It may signal that what has been declared is already beginning.  

**14:15** “Beyond the River”: The phrase ordinarily refers to the Euphrates. The prophecy envisions Israel’s removal from the land and scattering into exile.  

**14:23, 15** “Asherah poles”: These were cultic objects associated with Asherah worship. Their presence throughout the land represents a direct breach of exclusive devotion to the LORD.  

**14:24** “Male cult prostitutes”: The Hebrew term may denote males connected with prohibited cultic sexual practices. The verse places this practice among the abominations attributed to the nations displaced before Israel.  

**14:25** The name “Shishak” is written twice in the Hebrew textual tradition at this point; the translation gives it once.  

## Vocabulary

v14: 8 — מַמְלָכָה (*mamlakhah*)  
“kingdom.” The kingdom taken from David’s house and given to Jeroboam remains the LORD’s to give and to tear away.  

v14: 9 — מַסֵּכָה (*massekhah*)  
“cast image.” Jeroboam’s images are not neutral political symbols; they are portrayed as rival gods made to provoke the LORD.  

v14: 10 — בָּעַר (*baʿar*)  
“burn away, consume.” The verb frames the destruction of Jeroboam’s house as a thorough removal, reinforced by the image of dung burned until gone.  

v14: 13 — דָּבָר טוֹב (*davar tov*)  
“something good.” The phrase marks an unexpected moral and theological exception within Jeroboam’s condemned household.  

v14: 15 — נָתַשׁ (*natash*)  
“uproot.” Israel’s removal from the land is pictured not merely as defeat but as a plant torn from the soil given to its ancestors.  

v14: 16 — חָטָא (*ḥataʾ*)  
“sin; cause to sin.” Jeroboam’s guilt is both personal and communal: he sinned and led Israel into sin.  

v14: 23 — בָּמָה (*bamah*)  
“high place.” These local worship sites stand in contrast to Jerusalem, the city chosen for the LORD’s name.  

v14: 24 — קָדֵשׁ (*qadesh*)  
“male cult prostitute.” Though related in form to the word for holiness, the term here names a practice the text treats as an abomination.
