# Fluent Translation

## Purpose and posture

Fluent is a contemporary English translation of Scripture made for natural, sustained reading. It is intended for daily reading, devotion, worship, teaching, formation, and accessible group Bible study.

Fluent seeks to help readers become at home in the biblical story: able to follow its movement, hear its voices, recognize its recurring language, and carry its words into prayer and life. Clarity serves attention; it does not replace it.

> **Fluent — Natural Reading:** for daily reading, devotion, teaching, and growing biblical fluency.

## Source texts and translation relationship

Fluent is translated from verified source texts in the biblical languages:

- Hebrew and Aramaic for the Old Testament
- Koine Greek for the New Testament

No modern English translation functions as its base text. Other translations, including The Shared Word Translation (TSW), may be consulted as comparators during review, but they do not govern Fluent's wording.

Fluent and TSW are siblings within The Shared Word Project. Fluent is not a simplified edition of TSW. Each draws independently from the source texts and embodies a distinct reading posture.

## Natural English and faithful movement

Fluent gives priority to clear, idiomatic contemporary English while carrying the meaning, emphasis, relationships, and movement of the source text. A sentence may be restructured when source-language syntax would sound obscure or unnatural in English, but restructuring should not erase a significant connection or add an explanation that the text itself does not make.

The translation aims to be:

- clear without becoming thin;
- natural without becoming casual;
- reverent without sounding archaic;
- accessible without talking down to the reader;
- suitable for silent reading and reading aloud.

## Literary form and voice

Natural reading does not mean making every biblical voice sound the same. Narrative should move as narrative. Poetry should remain poetry. Wisdom, lament, prophecy, law, Gospel, letter, and apocalypse should retain their characteristic energy and form.

Repetition may be retained when it carries structure, emphasis, memory, or theological weight. It may be varied when reproducing it mechanically would obscure the function it has in the source language.

Headings, paragraphs, lineation, and punctuation are reading aids. They should help readers perceive the passage's movement without pretending to be part of the ancient text.

## Clarity, ambiguity, and restraint

Fluent normally makes relationships explicit enough for a contemporary reader to follow a passage naturally. It does not, however, resolve uncertainty simply because uncertainty is uncomfortable.

When the source text genuinely permits more than one consequential reading, Fluent may preserve the openness in the main text or name it briefly in a note. Clarity is not license to settle theological or interpretive questions that the source leaves open.

## Gender and human language

Fluent renders gendered language according to referent, context, literary force, and ordinary contemporary English.

When a source-language masculine expression clearly refers to a mixed group or to people generally, Fluent may use inclusive English. When gender is specific, narratively important, symbolically meaningful, or theologically consequential, it is retained. Decisions are contextual rather than governed by a blanket rule.

The aim is neither to impose a modern ideology on the text nor to preserve inherited English wording when it misstates who is being addressed.

## Notes and vocabulary

The main text should remain readable on its own. Notes and vocabulary are selective aids, not a running commentary.

Notes may identify:

- a significant textual variant;
- a source-language ambiguity or idiom;
- an interpretive possibility that materially affects translation;
- a literary, cultural, or grammatical feature needed to understand the rendering.

Vocabulary entries may identify recurring or consequential source-language terms. Technical detail should be concise and tied to public verse numbers.

Notes should not turn Fluent into a doctrinal argument, devotional guide, or substitute for the future Fluent Companion.

## Theological and communal posture

Fluent stands within the broad Christian tradition. It is made for readers and communities across theological difference who are willing to attend to Scripture with humility.

The translation should not resolve doctrinal disagreements by quietly loading one position into the English. It should not capture Scripture for a political party, flatten the testimony of people who suffer, or confuse neutrality with faithfulness. Where the text confronts power, names harm, protects dignity, welcomes the stranger, or attends to people at the margins, Fluent should let that witness be heard without turning translation into commentary.

## Formation rather than mastery

Biblical fluency is more than acquiring information. It grows as readers inhabit Scripture over time, recognize its patterns, bring their lives honestly before it, and learn to hear one another in its presence.

Fluent therefore serves a larger formational vision. The future Fluent Companion may add book and passage introductions, historical and literary context, themes, reflection, prayer, and embodied practices. Those resources should be trauma-aware, attentive to human dignity and affected voices, and hospitable to readers across the breadth of the church. They remain distinct from the translation itself.

## Ongoing work

Fluent is complete across all 66 books and remains provisional. It will be tested in reading, worship, teaching, and communal life. Corrections and careful revisions should be documented through releases rather than silently changing the canonical text.

The goal is not effortless reading. The goal is language clear enough that readers can give their effort to Scripture itself.
