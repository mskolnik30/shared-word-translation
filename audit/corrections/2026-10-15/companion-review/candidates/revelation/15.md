# Revelation 15

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

John sees a sign that introduces the final seven plagues. Before the bowls are poured out in the next chapter, this short vision includes a song associated with Moses and the Lamb. Read judgment, worship, and the nations together rather than treating the chapter only as a sequence of disasters.

### Chapter Path

- **15:1:** Seven angels appear with the last seven plagues.
- **15:2–4:** Those who have overcome the beast sing beside or on the sea of glass.
- **15:5–8:** The heavenly sanctuary opens, bowls are given, and smoke fills the temple.

### Watch For

Notice who acts, who sings, and to whom the song is addressed. The singers acclaim God's works and ways; the text does not command the reader to imitate the angels' actions.

## Read the Chapter

Read all eight verses in Fluent. Hear the song as a song, allowing its lines to interrupt a reading concerned only with what happens next.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How does the song change your hearing of the vision that surrounds it?

### The Chapter in Brief

John sees seven angels with seven plagues described as the last. Those who have overcome the beast, its image, and the number of its name stand at a sea of glass mixed with fire, holding harps. Their song names God's works as great and astonishing and God's ways as righteous and true. It anticipates all nations coming to worship. Angels then emerge from the opened heavenly sanctuary and receive seven bowls filled with God's fury. Smoke fills the temple, and no one can enter until the plagues are completed (15:1–8).

### Follow the Movement

The chapter places worship between the announcement of the plagues and the giving of the bowls. The song is not simply the celebration of a group's superiority over everyone else: it directs attention to God and names the nations' coming worship (15:2–4).

Moses and the Lamb are named together in connection with the song. The chapter invites attention to scriptural relationships without explaining every symbol. Its images of sea, sanctuary, testimony, linen, gold, and smoke should remain particular rather than becoming interchangeable decorations (15:2–8).

Judgment remains severe. Attending to its imagery does not require reducing it to a modern timetable or turning divine judgment into human permission to punish opponents. That restraint is an interpretive safeguard, not a claim that the chapter has no disturbing content.

### Threads Through Scripture

**Exodus 15:1–18** offers a useful comparison for Moses, song, sea, and deliverance. Compare the songs rather than assuming they have identical wording or circumstances. **Revelation 16:1–7** takes up the bowls and the language of righteous judgment, showing why this chapter's song belongs within a larger movement.

### Questions for Conversation

What does the song praise? What place do the nations have in it? How can a reader hear the longing for wrong to be answered without claiming the right to enact the vision's violence?

### Prayer and Practice

“God whose ways are righteous and true, receive our longing for justice and keep us from making ourselves its final judges.” You may name one wrong that needs truthful attention and one proportionate, nonviolent responsibility available to you. No one needs to disclose personal trauma in order to discuss the vision.
