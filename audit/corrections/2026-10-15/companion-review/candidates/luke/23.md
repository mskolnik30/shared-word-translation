# Luke 23

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

The council brings Jesus to Pilate. What follows is a sequence of accusations, declarations that he does not deserve death, public pressure, crucifixion, and burial. Luke places very different responses inside the same events. Watch people closely rather than assigning one response to an entire group.

### Chapter Path

- **23:1–12:** Jesus is questioned before Pilate and Herod.
- **23:13–25:** Pilate repeatedly rejects the capital charge but grants the demand for crucifixion.
- **23:26–32:** Simon carries the cross; Jesus addresses the mourning women.
- **23:33–43:** Jesus is crucified, prays for forgiveness, and answers a criminal's appeal.
- **23:44–56:** Jesus dies; witnesses respond; Joseph and the women attend to burial.

### Watch For

Distinguish accusations from the narrator's assertions. Notice who mocks, mourns, asks to be remembered, recognizes righteousness, or refuses consent to the council's action. The chapter itself prevents an undifferentiated account of collective responsibility.

## Read the Chapter

Read the complete chapter in Fluent, including its ending with the women's preparation and Sabbath rest. The numbering skips 23:17 in accordance with the bound source; the note makes that decision visible.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How does Luke carry the repeated declarations about Jesus' innocence or righteousness through to the responses at his death and burial?

### The Chapter in Brief

Pilate finds no basis for a charge; Herod questions Jesus, receives no answer, and mocks him. Back before Pilate, the demand for crucifixion prevails and Barabbas is released. Simon is compelled to carry the cross. Jesus addresses women mourning for him and warns of coming vulnerability. At the place of execution, he prays for forgiveness and promises paradise to the criminal who asks to be remembered. After Jesus dies, the centurion praises God, crowds mourn, and acquaintances watch. Joseph, who had not agreed with the council's action, buries Jesus. The women observe the tomb, prepare spices, and rest (23:1–56).

### Follow the Movement

Statements of innocence do not prevent violence. Pilate's willingness to have Jesus flogged and his eventual decision expose the gap between declaring a judgment and acting upon it (23:13–25).

Jesus' words to the women acknowledge the danger facing them and their children. They should not be turned into contempt for motherhood, infertility, or the mourners themselves. The bodily images belong to this warning within the narrative (23:27–31).

The final scenes preserve distinct acts of attention. Joseph dissents, asks for the body, and provides burial. The women see where and how it is laid. Neither their grief nor their rest is presented as the end of the story (23:49–56).

### Threads Through Scripture

**Luke 22:66–71** precedes the hearing before Pilate. **Luke 24:1–12** takes up the women's return to the tomb. Following these immediate connections prevents the account of death from being separated from either the proceedings before it or the witness after it.

### Questions for Conversation

Which judgments are spoken but not acted upon? Who refuses agreement with the council? What does the women's attention preserve for the next chapter? How can a group name wrongdoing without using Jesus' prayer to silence someone who was harmed?

### Prayer and Practice

“God of mercy, receive those who grieve and strengthen truthful care.” Offer a specific, voluntary act of support to someone facing loss. The prayer for forgiveness is not an instruction to force reconciliation, abandon justice, or remain in danger.

## Go Deeper

### Reading Carefully

The corrected note at 23:34 says that the bound SBLGNT includes Jesus' prayer without square doubt-brackets, while its apparatus records variation. Fluent retains the prayer without those brackets. This corrects the description of the source; it does not deny the existence of a textual question. At 23:47, Fluent's “righteous” belongs beside the repeated innocence declarations without exhausting the word's wider range.
