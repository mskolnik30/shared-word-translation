# Acts 9

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Saul begins this chapter seeking authority to arrest disciples. By its end, the narrative has moved through his encounter with Jesus, the fearful welcome of other disciples, Peter's healing of Aeneas, and the restoration of Tabitha. Do not let Saul's prominence make the other named people disappear.

### Chapter Path

- **9:1–9:** Saul's journey of persecution is interrupted.
- **9:10–19:** Ananias voices his fear, goes to Saul, and receives him.
- **9:20–25:** Saul proclaims Jesus and later escapes a plot against his life.
- **9:26–31:** Barnabas speaks for Saul before the apostles; the church's life continues.
- **9:32–35:** Peter meets Aeneas in Lydda.
- **9:36–43:** Tabitha's death, the widows' grief, and her restoration are narrated in Joppa.

### Watch For

Who is afraid, who speaks, who offers practical help, and who is named? Notice the contrast between Saul's plan to bring people bound to Jerusalem and his own need to be led by the hand into Damascus (9:2,8).

## Read the Chapter

Read the whole chapter in Fluent. Pay attention to food, hands, clothing, a bed, and a basket as well as to visions and proclamation.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How do Ananias, Barnabas, the disciples, and the widows contribute to a chapter often remembered mainly for Saul?

### The Chapter in Brief

Jesus confronts Saul on the road. Unable to see, Saul is led into Damascus. Ananias questions the command to visit someone known for harm, but goes, calls him brother, and lays hands on him. Saul regains sight, is baptized, eats, and stays with disciples. His proclamation astonishes hearers, and threats follow. Barnabas later explains Saul's encounter and activity to the apostles. Peter heals Aeneas, then is summoned after Tabitha dies. The widows show the clothing she made; Peter prays, and Tabitha is presented alive to the gathered community (9:1–43).

### Follow the Movement

Ananias's fear is spoken rather than hidden. The Lord answers his concern, and the narrative gives him a concrete part in Saul's reception. Later, the Jerusalem disciples are still afraid, and Barnabas provides testimony rather than demanding trust without explanation (9:10–18,26–27).

Tabitha is named as a disciple whose good works and care for the poor are already known. The widows' clothing gives material form to their testimony. Their grief matters within the account; Tabitha is not simply an occasion for displaying Peter's importance (9:36–41).

The stories connect extraordinary acts with ordinary care. Saul needs food and companions. Threatened disciples help him escape. People wash a body, send messengers, show garments, pray, and extend a hand. This is an observation about the chapter's actions, not a promise that every faithful community will experience the same outcomes.

### Threads Through Scripture

**Acts 8:3** supplies the earlier account of Saul's persecution. **Acts 22:6–16** retells the road encounter later in the narrative. Compare the accounts attentively without forcing every detail into one wording or assuming that the differences have already been resolved.

### Questions for Conversation

What makes the disciples' fear understandable? What does Barnabas actually tell the apostles? How do the widows remember Tabitha? Whose practical care would be missing from a retelling centered only on prominent leaders?

### Prayer and Practice

“Living Christ, give us truthful attention to harm and generous attention to care.” You may acknowledge someone whose work sustains others. Welcoming change does not require a harmed person to resume unsafe contact or treat a claimed transformation as sufficient evidence of safety.

## Go Deeper

### Reading Carefully

Both parts of 9:19 remain together: Saul regains strength through food and stays with the Damascus disciples. The correction removed only a second, unnumbered copy of the latter statement. The note at 9:7 preserves this account's description of what Saul's companions perceived; this Companion does not offer a new grammatical harmonization of the later retellings.
