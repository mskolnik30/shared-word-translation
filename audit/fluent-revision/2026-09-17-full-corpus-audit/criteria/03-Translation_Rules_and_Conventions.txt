Translation Rules & Conventions

1. Purpose of This Document

This document exists to safeguard consistency, restraint, and theological integrity throughout the translation process. It records the guiding commitments that shape decisions at every level—lexical, grammatical, literary, and theological—so that individual passages are not treated in isolation or according to momentary preference.

These rules are not exhaustive, nor are they mechanical. They articulate posture and priority, not algorithms. When tension arises between rules, judgment is exercised in keeping with the overall philosophy articulated in the Translator’s Preface.

⸻

2. Authority and Posture
	•	Scripture governs the translation, not vice versa.
	•	The goal is faithful rendering, not interpretive resolution.
	•	Translation is understood as an act of service to the church, not as a corrective to tradition or a vehicle for innovation.
	•	Ambiguity present in the source text is treated as meaningful and is preserved whenever possible.

When a decision feels difficult, the default question is not “What is clearest?” but “What is most faithful to what the text is doing?”

⸻

3. Source Texts

Old Testament
	•	Primary source: Masoretic Text
	•	Secondary witnesses (consulted where illuminating):
	•	Septuagint
	•	Dead Sea Scrolls
	•	Divergent readings of theological or interpretive significance are:
	•	noted rather than silently resolved
	•	reflected in footnotes where appropriate

New Testament
	•	Source: Critical Greek text reflecting best available manuscript evidence
	•	Significant textual variants are:
	•	acknowledged explicitly in notes
	•	not harmonized or obscured in the main text

⸻

4. Formal Approach

This translation follows a form-conscious approach.
	•	Grammatical structure is preserved where it carries meaning.
	•	Hebrew and Greek word order is respected but not reproduced mechanically.
	•	Rhetorical features (parallelism, repetition, chiasm, emphasis) are retained where discernible.
	•	Paraphrase is avoided.

When English clarity conflicts with formal preservation, minimal intervention is preferred.

⸻

5. Sentence Length and Syntax
	•	Long sentences are retained where English usage allows, especially in:
	•	epistolary material
	•	legal discourse
	•	theological argumentation
	•	Sentences may be divided only when:
	•	English comprehension breaks down
	•	referents become unclear
	•	Syntactic tension is not treated as a flaw to be corrected.

⸻

6. Vocabulary Choices
	•	Preference is given to common English words arranged with precision.
	•	Archaic language is avoided unless it preserves theological meaning that modern equivalents cannot carry.
	•	Contemporary idiom and slang are avoided.
	•	Traditional theological terms (e.g., justification, covenant, righteousness) are retained where they function meaningfully within the church’s theological vocabulary.

Lexical consistency is preferred, but not absolute. Context governs meaning.

⸻

7. Repetition
	•	Repetition in the source text is generally preserved.
	•	Repetition is assumed to be:
	•	deliberate
	•	rhetorically meaningful
	•	sometimes theologically significant

Variation for stylistic elegance is avoided unless repetition produces confusion in English.

⸻

8. Ambiguity
	•	Where the source text permits more than one plausible reading, ambiguity is preserved.
	•	Ambiguity is not resolved in favor of:
	•	later doctrinal developments
	•	harmonization with parallel texts
	•	perceived pastoral clarity
	•	Notes may acknowledge interpretive options, but the main text does not adjudicate them.

Ambiguity is treated as a feature of revelation, not a failure of translation.

⸻

9. Gendered Language (General)

Human Referents
	•	Gendered language is translated as written when referring to specific persons.
	•	Where the original language clearly intends:
	•	a collective
	•	a generic referent
gender-inclusive language may be used.

Decisions are contextual, not mechanical.

References to God
	•	Grammatical gender in the source languages is respected.
	•	Masculine pronouns are retained where:
	•	grammatically required
	•	rhetorically or poetically integral
	•	Proper names and titles (e.g., God, the LORD) are preferred over repeated pronouns where clarity allows.
	•	Feminine or plural pronouns for God are not introduced where the text does not use them.
	•	Biblical metaphors—including maternal and non-gendered imagery—are retained fully and without qualification.

The goal is fidelity to the text, not balance, correction, or expansion.

⸻

10. Names, Titles, and Divine Designations
	•	Divine names are translated consistently, with sensitivity to literary and theological context.
	•	Capitalization follows standard English conventions without theological signaling.
	•	Titles are not harmonized across contexts where the source text varies.

⸻

11. Poetry and Literary Form
	•	Poetry is formatted to reflect:
	•	lineation
	•	parallelism
	•	rhythm
	•	Metaphor is retained, even when difficult or unfamiliar.
	•	Explanatory additions are avoided.

Readers are trusted to engage imagery without mediation.

⸻

12. Notes and Apparatus
	•	Notes exist to:
	•	acknowledge textual variants
	•	identify significant interpretive possibilities
	•	Notes do not:
	•	explain theology
	•	resolve ambiguity
	•	defend translation choices unnecessarily

The main text remains primary.

⸻

13. Consistency and Review
	•	Translation decisions are reviewed for internal consistency across books.
	•	When a departure from established practice is necessary, it should be:
	•	rare
	•	deliberate
	•	documented in this file

This document may be amended, but only cautiously and infrequently.

⸻

14. Final Discipline

When uncertain, prefer:
	•	restraint over resolution
	•	faithfulness over fluency
	•	trust over control

The task is not to make scripture easier, but to make it available.
