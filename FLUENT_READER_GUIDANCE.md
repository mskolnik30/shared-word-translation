# Reading the Fluent Translation

Draft reader guidance · prepared for editorial review

## Translator's preface

Fluent seeks to let readers follow the movement of Scripture in natural contemporary English. It is intended for sustained reading, worship, teaching, and personal study. Its language aims to be clear and reverent without becoming archaic or casual.

Fluent's source is the Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek text. Its primary Old Testament base is the Masoretic Text; its New Testament base is the project's documented critical Greek text. Significant textual differences belong in the notes when they affect the reading. Fluent and The Shared Word (TSW) share a commitment to faithfulness while serving that commitment through distinct English styles. Fluent is not a simplified rewriting of TSW.

Natural English sometimes calls for a different sentence structure. A long sentence may be divided, an idiom expressed through its English sense, or an implicit grammatical relationship made clear. Such changes must preserve what the source says, how its parts relate, and where it places emphasis. They must not supply an explanation the source leaves unstated.

Fluency does not mean removing every difficulty. Scripture includes unfamiliar customs, compact poetry, disputed expressions, and accounts that do not agree in every detail. A translation should help readers encounter these features honestly. Where more than one reading matters, the wording or a brief note should leave the alternative visible.

The books also speak in different voices. A genealogy should not sound like a personal appeal, nor should a lament lose its urgency. Repeated words, images, promises, and responses often carry the structure of a passage. Their function matters even when natural English cannot reproduce every feature of the original wording.

## How to read this translation

Read a paragraph, scene, poem, or argument as a whole before treating an individual verse as a separate statement. Verse numbers help locate a passage; they do not always mark a complete sentence or thought. Chapter divisions can also interrupt a continuing scene or argument.

Section headings are editorial guides. They should describe the passage without settling a question that the text leaves open. Poetry uses line breaks to help readers hear its movement, but its meaning still depends on the surrounding lines.

“LORD” represents the divine name under the translation's naming convention. Other divine names and titles retain their distinct functions. Capitalization is an English presentation choice and should not be treated as an additional claim about the source.

Words for a mixed or general human group may be rendered inclusively when context supports that meaning. A specific person, a significant gender distinction, or a meaningful image is preserved. References to God follow the source's language and images, including maternal images where they occur.

The notes are selective. They identify significant textual variants, alternative readings, difficult idioms, wordplay, and other features needed to understand a translation choice. “Another reading” may concern the meaning of the same source wording; a textual variant concerns a difference among witnesses. These are different kinds of uncertainty.

Vocabulary entries offer contextual help with selected Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek words. A word's possible meanings are not all present every time it occurs. Nor does a single English equivalent necessarily fit every setting.

Notes and vocabulary are reading aids, not a running sermon or commentary. Further interpretation and application belong in the Companion and in the reader's wider study. Shared wording with another translation is not automatically a problem: a familiar saying or concise phrase may be the clearest faithful English. Fluent's distinctiveness should come from its sustained voice and source-grounded choices.

This guidance states the edition's aims. The current manuscript remains an editorial draft; completion of its linguistic review, independent review, reader testing, and final approval is recorded separately in the editorial register.
