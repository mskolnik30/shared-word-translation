# The Name Given in the Work of Liberation

## First Look

Moses asks what he should say when the Israelites ask for the name of the God who sent him. God answers with the Hebrew *ehyeh asher ehyeh*, then tells Moses to say that *ehyeh* has sent him. The next verse identifies the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob by the four-letter divine name often written YHWH and rendered in English Bibles as “the LORD.”

The name is disclosed inside a larger declaration: God has seen forced labor, heard the oppressed people's cry, known their suffering, and commissioned Moses to confront Pharaoh. The answer is not an abstract riddle detached from history. It is bound to presence, memory, and liberation.

## Why This Matters

The phrase in verse 14 has shaped Jewish reverence for the divine name, Christian teaching about God's being, philosophical accounts of existence, and liberation theology. English “I AM WHO I AM” is familiar and defensible, but Hebrew verbal aspect also permits “I WILL BE WHO I WILL BE” or “I WILL BE WHAT I WILL BE.” The choice affects whether readers first hear timeless being, sovereign freedom, promised presence, or some combination.

The passage also demands care because Christians have often treated Jewish avoidance of pronouncing YHWH as a problem to overcome. It is instead a living practice of reverence. Public materials should normally follow the translation's “LORD,” explain YHWH when needed, and never claim certainty about the ancient pronunciation beyond the evidence.

## Look Closely

The Hebrew verb *hayah* means “be,” “become,” or “happen.” *Ehyeh* is its first-person form and can be heard as present or future according to context. Only one verse earlier God promises Moses, *ehyeh immak*—“I will be with you.” That echo makes promised presence central to the name-answer: the God who sends Moses will be with him.

The relative word *asher* can mean “who,” “that,” or “what,” leaving several possible renderings. The phrase may reveal while also refusing to place God under human control. A name is given, but Moses does not receive a definition that masters its bearer.

The relation between *ehyeh* and YHWH is suggested by sound and verbal association, yet it is not a simple one-to-one grammatical conversion. Verse 15 immediately grounds the divine name in ancestral relationship and future memory: this is the God of the ancestors, remembered through generations.

The Greek Septuagint rendered the phrase *ego eimi ho on*, roughly “I am the one who is.” That translation became enormously influential for Christian reflection on God as true being. It is a major reception of the Hebrew, not the only possible lexical translation of it.

## More Than One Reading

Metaphysical readings hear a declaration of self-existence, constancy, or the One whose being depends on nothing else. Promise-centered readings hear “I will be with you as I will be,” connecting the answer to God's active presence. Freedom-centered readings hear a refusal to be contained: God will be who God will be. Some interpreters think the phrase is deliberately elusive because Moses' request cannot reduce God to a usable label.

These readings overlap. The living God is not controlled by Pharaoh, Moses, or an interpreter; the same God binds divine identity to the suffering people and to the promise of presence.

## Across Christian History

Jewish reading customarily substitutes *Adonai* (“Lord”) when the four-letter name appears in Scripture or prayer; *HaShem* (“the Name”) is a common reverential way to refer to God in ordinary speech or study. Practices vary by community, but neither term attempts to pronounce the written name. The name functions within covenant, prayer, holiness, and memory rather than as a password.

Patristic theologians, drawing on the Septuagint and Greek philosophy, found here a scriptural grammar for God's fullness of being. Augustine and Aquinas later related the passage to divine immutability and the distinction between Creator and creatures. Reformers retained the emphasis on God's self-existence while also stressing covenant faithfulness.

Modern Jewish and Christian interpreters have renewed attention to the future force of *ehyeh*. Liberation theologians rightly return verse 14 to verses 7–12: God's identity is known in hearing the cry of enslaved people and acting against oppression.

## Power and Consequences

“I AM” must not become an authoritarian slogan attached to a leader, nation, or institution. The name belongs to the God who sees what taskmasters do and sends a reluctant shepherd against imperial power.

Liberation language also requires discipline. The Israelites' deliverance cannot make another people's dehumanization acceptable, and modern communities should not simply cast themselves as Israel and their opponents as Egypt. Analogy must be tested by whose suffering is seen, whose testimony is heard, and what power is being confronted.

Reverence for the name should lead to restraint. Speaking confidently about God is not the same as possessing God.

## Consider Together

> **Questions for public reflection**
>
> - What changes when verse 14 is heard as “I am” and when it is heard as “I will be”?
> - How does the promise “I will be with you” in verse 12 shape the answer in verse 14?
> - Why does verse 15 immediately connect the name with ancestors and generations?
> - What practices honor Jewish reverence for the written name?
> - Where does a claimed knowledge of God resist oppression, and where can it become a tool of control?
