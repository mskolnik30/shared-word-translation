# Image, Dominion, and Human Vocation

## First Look

At the climax of Genesis 1, God creates *humankind* in the divine image and likeness, names humanity as male and female, blesses them, and commissions them to fill the earth and exercise dominion among other living creatures. The text gives humanity a distinctive vocation inside a creation already pronounced good.

The poem moves between collective and singular language: humankind is created; “him” is created; “male and female” are created; “them” are blessed. The image is not restricted to a ruler, a sex, an ethnicity, a capacity, or a social class. It is a declaration about humanity.

## Why This Matters

These verses have supported strong claims about human dignity, equality, sexuality, family, political rule, technology, and the environment. They have also been recruited to rank some humans above others and to treat the nonhuman world as raw material. Much depends on what “image,” “subdue,” and “have dominion” are understood to mean.

The passage offers no checklist of abilities that make a person God's image. Defining the image solely as rationality, speech, autonomy, physical form, or productivity can exclude infants, people with cognitive disabilities, and anyone measured by another person's ideal of capacity. The text grants identity before it describes achievement.

## Look Closely

The Hebrew *adam* in this setting means humankind or humanity, not only the later individual named Adam. The terms *tselem* (“image”) and *demut* (“likeness”) overlap. Images in the ancient world could represent a deity or extend a king's presence and authority. Genesis makes an arresting move: the representative vocation associated with kings is given to humanity.

“Let us make” has received several explanations: address within a divine council, a plural of deliberation, an address to heavenly beings, or a reading later Christians relate to the Trinity. The plural should not be made to carry more than it can. Verse 27 returns to singular divine action: God creates.

The verbs *radah* (“have dominion”) and *kabash* (“subdue”) are genuinely strong. They should not be softened into words that mean only gentle care. Yet their meaning is bounded by the chapter: the ruler whose image humans bear creates, orders, blesses, provides food, and calls the whole community of life good. Dominion is delegated, creaturely rule; it is not ownership independent of God.

Verse 27's compact poetry binds the image to all humanity and places “male and female” in parallel. The line does not assign dominion to males over females. Nor does the blessing to be fruitful turn reproduction into a test of individual faithfulness. It addresses the human community as a whole and is first spoken as blessing.

## More Than One Reading

Substantive readings locate the image in a human quality, often reason, freedom, relationality, or spiritual capacity. Functional readings emphasize humanity's royal task of representing God's rule. Relational readings locate the image in life before God and with others. Vocational readings combine identity and task: humans are created as God's representatives and therefore summoned into Godlike rule.

Some Christian traditions have distinguished “image” from “likeness,” sometimes treating one as given and the other as a goal of transformation. Many contemporary interpreters see the two terms as poetic parallels. The theological distinction may be fruitful as reception, but it is difficult to prove from the Hebrew pair alone.

Ecological interpreters differ over whether dominion can be recovered as responsible governance or whether the royal metaphor remains too dangerous. The text's history of use must remain part of that judgment.

## Across Christian History

Irenaeus connected image and likeness with humanity's growth toward communion with God. Augustine often emphasized the mind's reflection of the triune God. Eastern Christian traditions developed the image within participation and deification; Reformation traditions stressed both enduring dignity and the distortion of human vocation by sin.

Modern interpretation has reclaimed the royal and representative setting of the image. Feminist readers have emphasized that male and female equally bear it. Black, postcolonial, and liberation theologians have appealed to the image against racial hierarchy and dehumanization while also exposing the contradiction between Christian confession and colonial practice. Disability theologians press the church to confess the image without making worth depend on socially prized capacities.

## Power and Consequences

The image of God denies every ideology that grades human beings by race, sex, disability, nationality, wealth, usefulness, or religious power. It also places human authority under judgment: a representative may not treat the represented world as personal property.

Dominion has been used to justify extraction, animal suffering, seizure of land, and disregard for climate. A responsible reading does not pretend those effects disappear when the term is translated more gently. It asks what kind of rule faithfully images the Creator described in the whole chapter.

The blessing of fruitfulness should never be weaponized against people who are infertile, celibate, unmarried, childless, queer, or unable to conform to a community's family ideal. The corporate blessing is not a command that every body reproduce.
