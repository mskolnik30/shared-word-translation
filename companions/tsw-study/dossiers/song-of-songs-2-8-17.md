# Desire Speaks

## First Look

A woman hears her beloved approaching and quotes his invitation to rise and come away in spring. She answers with mutual belonging, desire, and an enigmatic appeal concerning foxes and vineyards.

## Why This Matters

This passage bears directly on Speaker changes are not marked in the Hebrew text and must be inferred. 'My beloved is mine and I am his' expresses reciprocity, while the final verse's mountains may be mountains of separation or a sensuous image.. Translation and interpretation therefore affect not only ideas but the treatment of real people and communities. A responsible reading should identify what the text says, what readers infer, and where later institutions have made the passage do additional work.

## Look Closely

The poem delights in anticipation, voice, landscape, and embodied desire without narrating marriage, procreation, or male command. Its images should remain poetry rather than be forced into a single code.

Read the passage within its whole literary unit before isolating a single phrase. TSW's visible repetitions and tensions are evidence for study rather than problems to harmonize prematurely.

## More Than One Reading

Literal readings celebrate human erotic love; Jewish allegory hears God's love for Israel; Christian allegory hears Christ and church or soul. Queer readings explore desire that exceeds later institutional scripts.

These positions should be compared by their linguistic evidence, historical assumptions, canonical relationships, and practical consequences. Naming several readings does not imply that every reading is equally persuasive or safe.

## Across Christian History

Rabbinic tradition defended the Song's holiness, and mystical traditions made it central to divine love. Modern recovery of its erotic sense corrects traditions that treated bodies as embarrassing, but allegory remains a real reception history.

Reception history shows what communities noticed and what they overlooked. It is evidence about interpretation, not an automatic verdict that the oldest, newest, or most powerful reading is correct.

## Power and Consequences

Celebrating desire does not erase consent, difference, or the possibility of rejection. Romantic preaching should not pressure people toward partnership or treat singleness, asexuality, or nonconforming bodies as incomplete.

Interpretation should be evaluated in the presence of people who bear its consequences. Safeguarding, truthful history, and accountable power are theological concerns rather than optional additions.
