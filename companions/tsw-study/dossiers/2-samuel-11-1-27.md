# A King Takes

## First Look

David sees Bathsheba, sends messengers, takes her, and sleeps with her. When she reports pregnancy, he tries to manipulate Uriah and then arranges Uriah's death.

## Why This Matters

This passage bears directly on The repeated royal verbs—David sends and takes—drive the account. The narrator gives no courtship, no request from Bathsheba, and no indication that refusal was safe; modern claims of mutual seduction import details the text withholds.. Translation and interpretation therefore affect not only ideas but the treatment of real people and communities. A responsible reading should identify what the text says, what readers infer, and where later institutions have made the passage do additional work.

## Look Closely

Bathsheba's bathing follows purification after menstruation, not an invitation to be watched. The narrative's moral verdict arrives plainly: what David had done was evil in the LORD's eyes.

Read the passage within its whole literary unit before isolating a single phrase. TSW's visible repetitions and tensions are evidence for study rather than problems to harmonize prematurely.

## More Than One Reading

Traditional readings often called the episode adultery; trauma-aware and feminist readings name abuse of royal power or rape. Legal categories vary, but the power imbalance and David's escalating concealment are central literary facts.

These positions should be compared by their linguistic evidence, historical assumptions, canonical relationships, and practical consequences. Naming several readings does not imply that every reading is equally persuasive or safe.

## Across Christian History

Rabbinic texts debate David's culpability yet preserve sharp criticism. Christian art and preaching frequently eroticized Bathsheba and transferred blame to her. Womanist interpreters restore attention to the woman whose speech is nearly erased.

Reception history shows what communities noticed and what they overlooked. It is evidence about interpretation, not an automatic verdict that the oldest, newest, or most powerful reading is correct.

## Power and Consequences

No appeal to David's later repentance should minimize Bathsheba's harm or Uriah's death. Communities should name abuse by admired leaders, preserve evidence, protect affected people, and reject institutional strategies that prioritize reputation.

Interpretation should be evaluated in the presence of people who bear its consequences. Safeguarding, truthful history, and accountable power are theological concerns rather than optional additions.
