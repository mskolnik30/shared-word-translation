# Abandonment, Deliverance, and Christian Reception

## First Look

Psalm 22 begins with abandonment: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” The speaker remembers ancestral trust yet experiences unanswered crying, bodily collapse, public mockery, and surrounding violence. Then the prayer turns. The speaker anticipates or announces deliverance, praises God in the assembly, calls the afflicted to eat, and imagines the nations and future generations remembering the LORD.

The psalm is not only despair and not only victory. Its praise emerges from lament that has been spoken in full. Christian readers hear Jesus' crucifixion throughout the psalm because the Gospel passion narratives use its words and images. That reception is indispensable for Christianity, but it must not erase the psalm as Jewish Scripture and prayer.

## Why This Matters

Psalm 22 gives religious language to people who feel abandoned by God without requiring them to stop addressing God. It has offered words in illness, persecution, grief, and death. It has also been made into a prediction chart in which every line is detached from the psalm's own speaker and Israel's worship.

The passage's textual history is especially visible in the disputed line concerning hands and feet. Popular Christian claims sometimes present one reading as obviously original and accuse Jewish tradition of concealment. The evidence does not justify that polemic.

## Look Closely

The opening address holds protest and relationship together: the absent one is still “my God.” Alternation between complaint, remembered trust, petition, and praise creates the psalm's movement. The speaker's body is not decorative imagery; bones, heart, mouth, tongue, hands, and feet make suffering embodied.

Verse numbering differs between Hebrew and many English traditions, so source references around the hands-and-feet line may appear one number apart.

The Masoretic Text is commonly vocalized “like a lion, my hands and my feet,” a difficult expression because it lacks an obvious verb. The Greek Septuagint reads “they dug” or “pierced” my hands and feet. Manuscript evidence from the Judean Desert is frequently cited in the discussion, but damaged letters and competing reconstructions require caution. “Pierced” has ancient support; “like a lion” is not a late Jewish plot; and the precise earliest form remains disputed.

The turn toward praise does not necessarily mark a simple emotional improvement. It may reflect confidence that prayer has been heard, a later thanksgiving performed after rescue, or a liturgical movement in which lament and public testimony belong together.

## More Than One Reading

Historical readings ask what crisis an Israelite speaker or worshiping community voices. Royal readings hear a suffering king. Corporate readings hear Israel's suffering concentrated in one voice. Christian christological reading hears Jesus faithfully inhabiting Israel's prayer and the Gospel writers portraying the cross through it.

These readings can be related without claiming that the psalm had no meaning until the crucifixion. Matthew and Mark quote its opening; other passion details echo mockery, divided garments, and public spectacle. The Gospels interpret Jesus through the psalm, and Jesus' use of its opening can summon the whole lament-to-praise movement without making his anguish unreal.

## Across Christian History

The early church read Psalm 22 as a privileged witness to Christ's passion. Patristic interpretation often placed the words on Christ's lips while also hearing the church as Christ's body. Medieval liturgy and art deepened the association.

Reformation commentators defended christological reading while attending to David's own suffering. Modern historical criticism re-centered the psalm's genre and ancient worship. Jewish interpretation continues to read it within Israel's Scriptures, frequently in relation to individual or communal affliction. Responsible Christian reading receives Jesus through Israel's prayer rather than taking the prayer away from Israel.

Liberation, Black, womanist, disability, and trauma-informed interpreters have emphasized the truth-telling body, public humiliation, and the difference between joining the afflicted in lament and explaining suffering from a safe distance.

## Power and Consequences

The movement to praise should not be used to hurry grieving people, demand optimism, or imply that faithful prayer always produces an immediate felt rescue. The psalm itself gives sustained space to abandonment.

Christian interpretation becomes harmful when it turns a textual variant into an accusation against Jews or treats Jewish reading as blindness. It also becomes harmful when the suffering body is romanticized. The psalm asks the assembly to hear the afflicted and participate in praise and provision; it does not invite spectatorship.

## Consider Together

> **Questions for public reflection**
>
> - What allows the speaker to protest abandonment while continuing to say “my God”?
> - Where does the psalm turn, and what explanations for that turn remain possible?
> - How do the Gospels read Jesus through Psalm 22?
> - What is lost if Christian readers treat the psalm only as prediction?
> - How can a community make room for lament without forcing a quick resolution?
> - What responsibilities accompany hearing another person's embodied suffering?
