# A Law That Cannot Be Made Safe

## First Look

This law concerns a man who encounters an unbetrothed virgin, “seizes her, and lies with her,” and is discovered. He must pay her father fifty shekels of silver, she becomes his wife, and he may never send her away. The woman is acted upon throughout. The passage reports no consent, no opportunity for refusal, and no penalty paid to her.

The law belongs to an ancient social world in which a daughter's sexual status affected her father's household and her prospects for economic security. Naming that setting explains features of the rule; it does not make the arrangement just or safe for a survivor.

## Why This Matters

Some readers have claimed that the Bible commands a rape survivor to marry her attacker. Others argue that the verbs may describe illicit sex or seduction rather than violent rape. The linguistic question is real, but neither solution should obscure the woman's vulnerability or turn an ancient remedy into present-day policy.

This passage has pastoral consequences. It must never be used to pressure a survivor toward marriage, reconciliation, silence, or continued proximity to an offender. Contemporary safeguarding requires safety, truthful reporting, survivor agency, and accountability.

## Look Closely

The Hebrew verb translated “seizes” is *tapas*. It can denote taking hold, capturing, or grasping, but by itself does not specify every circumstance of force or consent. The nearby law in Deuteronomy 22:25–27 uses a different verb and explicitly describes a betrothed woman being overpowered in the countryside. Exodus 22:16–17 addresses a man who “seduces” an unbetrothed virgin and allows the father to refuse the marriage. Comparison sharpens the questions, but scholars disagree about how the laws relate and whether Deuteronomy 22:28–29 assumes rape, seduction, or a broader category of illicit intercourse.

“They are found” makes public discovery decisive in the law's stated procedure. The payment compensates the father for the social and economic loss assigned to him in this patriarchal system. The permanent marriage obligation may have sought to prevent the man from abandoning a woman whose prospects had been damaged. Yet the text does not state the woman's preference, and permanent access to the man could compound rather than repair violence.

## More Than One Reading

One reading treats the law as a limited protection within an unjust economy: the man cannot use and discard the woman, and he bears a lasting material obligation. A second emphasizes the verbal and contextual evidence for sexual assault and judges the remedy itself as another injury. A third distinguishes the passage from the explicit rape case in verses 25–27 and reads it as seduction or consensual but unauthorized sex.

These readings are not morally equivalent simply because each has linguistic arguments. Even under a seduction reading, the woman's consent is not the legal center. Historical description and canonical interpretation must be distinguished from moral authorization.

## Across Christian History

Jewish interpretation did not simply repeat the law as a timeless command. The Temple Scroll and rabbinic traditions read and coordinated the Deuteronomic and Exodus laws in different ways. Later halakhic interpretation surrounded biblical rules with procedures and distinctions not visible in these two verses.

Christian interpreters have often read Israel's civil laws as historically particular while seeking moral principles within them. That move can expose the man's obligation, but it can also allow interpreters to praise incremental protection without listening to the person most at risk. Feminist and trauma-aware readings insist that the woman's missing voice is itself evidence readers must not fill with imagined consent.

## Power and Consequences

The passage places sexual status, money, marriage, and household authority together. A community reading it responsibly must refuse the idea that payment transfers ownership of a survivor or that marriage resolves sexual violence. Clergy, families, and institutions must not prioritize reputation, family unity, or an offender's restoration over a survivor's safety.

The law can be studied as part of Scripture without being reenacted as Christian ethics. Its moral pressure may lie partly in forcing readers to see how legal systems can restrain one form of male exploitation while preserving another.
