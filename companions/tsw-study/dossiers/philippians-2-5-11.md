# Power That Does Not Exploit

## First Look

Philippians 2:5–11 traces a startling movement. Christ Jesus is described in relation to divine form and equality, yet he does not treat that status as an opportunity for exploitation. He takes the form of a slave, enters human life, accepts humiliation, and becomes obedient to death on a cross. God then exalts him, and creation confesses his lordship.

The movement is not included as a detached lesson in doctrine. Paul introduces it after asking the Philippians to resist rivalry and empty glory, attend to one another's interests, and share one mind in Christ. The passage therefore speaks about who Christ is and about the kind of common life his story makes possible.

## Why This Matters

This passage has shaped Christian teaching about incarnation, Christ's divinity and humanity, humility, obedience, and the cross. It has also been used to demand self-erasure from people already denied dignity or safety. Reading carefully requires a distinction between freely refusing to exploit power and being forced to submit to another person's exploitation.

The crucified person described here is not praised because humiliation is good. Roman crucifixion was a brutal public punishment and execution shaped by social status. In the Republican period it was used especially against enslaved people; under the Empire it was applied more broadly to freedpeople and noncitizens, while Roman citizens were ordinarily protected from it. The passage announces that Jesus' self-giving path, rather than imperial domination, reveals true lordship—and that God vindicates the one Rome shamed.

## Look Closely

The opening command can be read as “let this mind be in you” or, more relationally, “cultivate this pattern among you.” The plural setting matters: Paul is forming a community, not prescribing a private technique of self-denial.

The rare noun *harpagmos* in verse 6 is the passage's sharpest lexical dispute. Older readings sometimes treated equality with God as something Christ did not seize. Other readings take it as something he already possessed but did not exploit for advantage. TSW chooses the latter: “did not regard equality with God as something to be exploited.” The decision fits the movement from status to self-giving, but the history of debate belongs in view.

“Emptied himself” does not specify a divine quality that Christ discarded. The sentence explains the emptying through participles: taking slave form and becoming human. The Greek *doulos* is stronger than the polite English “servant”; it names an enslaved person in a world structured by ownership and status. The text should not make slavery noble. It uses the lowest recognized social form to expose the direction of Christ's action.

The passage is often called a hymn. Its elevated rhythm and balanced movement support that description, but scholars disagree about whether Paul quotes an earlier composition, adapts traditional material, or writes exalted prose himself. The theological force does not depend on proving a pre-Pauline source.

## More Than One Reading

One major reading begins with Christ's preexistent divine status: he possesses equality with God, refuses to use it for advantage, becomes human, and is exalted after the cross. Another emphasizes an Adam pattern: unlike Adam, who reached toward divine status, Christ obeys. These need not exclude one another, but they organize the passage differently.

Interpreters also differ over whether the “name above every name” is the name Jesus or the divine title Lord. The allusion to Isaiah 45:23, where every knee bows to Israel's God, gives the confession “Jesus Christ is Lord” extraordinary weight.

Some read the language as a direct challenge to Roman imperial claims. Philippi was a Roman colony saturated with status and allegiance, and “Lord” language could carry political pressure. The contrast is plausible and illuminating, but it should not be presented as a certainty that exhausts the passage.

## Across Christian History

Early Christian theologians drew on this passage when confessing Christ as fully divine and fully human. The Council of Chalcedon's account of one Christ in two natures belongs to this reception history, not to a replacement of Paul's language with later formulas.

John Chrysostom used the passage against readings that diminished Christ's divine status. Reformers such as Calvin treated the emptying as concealment or humble restraint rather than the loss of divinity. Modern “kenotic” theologies have explored divine self-limitation more strongly, while feminist theologians have warned that an unqualified ideal of self-emptying can reinforce the disappearance of those whose selves have already been denied.

## Power and Consequences

Christ's pattern judges power used for self-protection, prestige, or domination. It does not authorize leaders to keep power while asking everyone else to become small. The one with status relinquishes advantage; the vulnerable person is not instructed to become more available for harm.

“Obedient to the point of death” must not become a command to remain in abuse, accept preventable violence, or obey a human authority without limit. Christian communities should ask who is being invited to give, who is doing the asking, and whether the supposed sacrifice serves another person's control.

The passage also unsettles success-centered faith. God's exaltation does not retroactively call the cross pleasant or erase its violence. It declares that imperial shame does not have the final word about Jesus.

## Consider Together

> **Questions for public reflection**
>
> - What does Christ possess in verse 6, and what does he refuse to do with it?
> - How does “slave” change the social force of the passage compared with “servant”?
> - Where have calls to humility protected a community, and where have they protected powerful people?
> - What would non-exploitative power look like in a church, family, workplace, or public institution?
> - How do verses 9–11 change—or fail to change—the scandal of verses 7–8?
