# A Woman of Strength

## First Look

An alphabetic poem praises an *eshet hayil*: a woman of strength, capability, or valor. She manages land, trade, textiles, household provision, generosity, and wise speech.

## Why This Matters

This passage bears directly on *Hayil* can mean strength, wealth, competence, or valor and is not adequately captured by passive 'virtue.' The acrostic's completeness is poetic praise, not a daily checklist for every woman.. Translation and interpretation therefore affect not only ideas but the treatment of real people and communities. A responsible reading should identify what the text says, what readers infer, and where later institutions have made the passage do additional work.

## Look Closely

Her work is public and economic as well as domestic. Servants and resources make the household's class position visible, while the poem's final command directs the community to praise her work at the gates.

Read the passage within its whole literary unit before isolating a single phrase. TSW's visible repetitions and tensions are evidence for study rather than problems to harmonize prematurely.

## More Than One Reading

Some read the poem as a model wife; Jewish reception also sings it as praise of a particular woman and allegorizes Torah or wisdom. Feminist readings find agency and economic skill while questioning impossible expectations and invisible labor.

These positions should be compared by their linguistic evidence, historical assumptions, canonical relationships, and practical consequences. Naming several readings does not imply that every reading is equally persuasive or safe.

## Across Christian History

The poem is recited in many Jewish homes on Sabbath eve. Christian women's teaching has sometimes turned it into a burden of perfection, while other readers reclaim its language of strength and public honor.

Reception history shows what communities noticed and what they overlooked. It is evidence about interpretation, not an automatic verdict that the oldest, newest, or most powerful reading is correct.

## Power and Consequences

No woman should be measured against every task in the poem or blamed when work, disability, poverty, singleness, or family form differs. Praise should lead to shared labor and material recognition, not sentimental exploitation.

Interpretation should be evaluated in the presence of people who bear its consequences. Safeguarding, truthful history, and accountable power are theological concerns rather than optional additions.
