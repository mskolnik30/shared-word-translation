# Mary, Martha, and the Work of Discipleship

## First Look

Martha welcomes Jesus into her home. Her sister Mary sits at the Lord's feet and listens to his word. Martha is distracted by much serving and asks Jesus to tell Mary to help. Jesus responds tenderly—“Martha, Martha”—yet says she is anxious and troubled about many things, while one thing is needed and Mary has chosen the good portion.

The scene is often reduced to action versus contemplation. Luke's wording and literary context make the contrast less simple: hospitality is good, serving is central to Jesus' mission, and sitting at a teacher's feet is the posture of a disciple.

## Why This Matters

The passage has honored women's access to theological learning. It has also produced stereotypes: Martha as fussy homemaker, Mary as spiritually superior, and domestic labor as lesser work. Women have been told both to serve constantly and to feel ashamed when that service becomes exhausting.

The interpretive question is not whether meals matter. It is whether service has become anxious, isolating, or compulsory, and whether every person is permitted to receive the word rather than being assigned to support someone else's discipleship.

## Look Closely

Martha is the one who welcomes Jesus; the house is associated with her agency. The noun *diakonia* can mean service or ministry and is not limited to cooking. The verb translated “distracted” suggests being pulled away. Luke does not call the work sinful.

Mary sits “at the Lord's feet,” language used for a learner's relation to a teacher. Her “portion” can evoke a chosen share, perhaps with scriptural echoes of God as one's portion. The textual tradition varies between “one thing is needed” and readings that speak of “few things, or one,” possibly relating the response to the meal. The shorter form sharpens the focus without identifying a single task.

Placed after the Good Samaritan, the scene should not oppose love of neighbor to attention to God. Luke presents active mercy and receptive listening beside one another.

## More Than One Reading

The classic contemplative reading places Mary above Martha as contemplation above action. A discipleship reading emphasizes that Jesus protects a woman's right to learn. A narrative reading holds the Samaritan and sisters together: love of neighbor and hearing the word correct one another. A labor reading asks who performs the work that makes another's contemplation possible.

Some feminist interpreters celebrate Mary's boundary crossing; others warn that blaming Martha repeats contempt for feminized labor. Recent work situates Martha's anxiety within ancient servile labor and dependence rather than an individual personality flaw.

## Across Christian History

Origen, Augustine, Gregory the Great, and later monastic traditions used Mary and Martha to discuss contemplative and active lives. Medieval readers sometimes assigned both to necessary forms of Christian vocation while still ranking contemplation higher. Reformation traditions stressed hearing the word but often retained gendered domestic ideals.

Modern feminist interpretation has recovered both women: Mary as disciple and Martha as host, minister, and truth-teller elsewhere in Christian memory. Ecumenical reading can refuse the competition that turns two women into permanent personality types.

## Power and Consequences

Necessary labor is often invisible until it stops. Churches can praise service while expecting women, poor people, or racialized workers to provide it without voice, rest, or formation. The passage must not be used to spiritualize exploitative workloads.

Jesus addresses Martha's anxiety without discarding her. A healthy community shares material work and ensures that those who serve also have protected access to learning, prayer, leadership, and rest.

## Consider Together

> **Questions for public reflection**
>
> - What does Martha do well before the conflict begins?
> - What is Mary claiming by sitting at the teacher's feet?
> - Is Jesus criticizing service, distraction, coercion, or something else?
> - Whose labor makes other people's study and worship possible?
> - How can service and attention become shared practices rather than gendered roles?
