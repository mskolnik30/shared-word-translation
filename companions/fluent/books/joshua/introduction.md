# Joshua book guide

## Begin Here

Joshua tells of Israel crossing the Jordan, entering Canaan, receiving land, and renewing covenant commitment. It is a book of fulfilled promise and courageous trust, but also of warfare and destruction that modern readers should not soften or imitate. The narrative belongs to Israel's particular ancient story; it is not a timeless authorization for conquest.

## At a Glance

- **Kind of writing:** Theological history, battle narrative, land lists, covenant ceremony, and farewell speech.
- **Primary setting:** Canaan, from the Jordan Valley to the hill country and tribal territories.
- **Central movement:** Israel moves from wilderness waiting to life in the land under Joshua.
- **People to know:** Joshua, Rahab, Caleb, Achan, Eleazar, tribal leaders, and the Gibeonites.
- **Time to read aloud:** About 2½ hours.

## What Is Going On?

Joshua succeeds Moses, and the opening scenes deliberately echo the exodus. The Jordan parts, a memorial is raised, and Israel is called to remember God's action. Rahab, a Canaanite woman, protects Israel's messengers and joins the people. Her presence complicates any reading that treats ethnic identity alone as the story's moral dividing line.

Accounts of cities placed under total destruction are among Scripture's most difficult passages. The book presents judgment, promise, and land within an ancient theological frame, while its battle summaries and later lists also show that occupation was neither as simple nor as complete as some statements sound. Faithful reading should name the violence, attend to victims who are not given speech, and reject using Joshua to sanctify modern displacement or war.

## The Book's Movement

1. **Chapters 1–5 — Entering the land:** Joshua is commissioned; Rahab protects the spies; Israel crosses the Jordan and prepares at Gilgal.
2. **Chapters 6–12 — Conflict and conquest:** Jericho falls; Achan's offense brings communal disaster; campaigns and victories follow.
3. **Chapters 13–21 — Receiving territories:** Land is allotted, refuge cities are appointed, and Levites receive towns.
4. **Chapters 22–24 — Holding together:** A disputed altar nearly divides Israel; Joshua gives final speeches and renews the covenant.

## Watch For

- Repeated echoes of Moses and the exodus.
- Memorials that teach future generations.
- Outsiders who respond faithfully and insiders who violate covenant.
- The difference between gift, responsibility, and possession.
- Unfinished work beneath sweeping victory language.

## A Thread to Carry

Joshua asks what it means to receive a gift without forgetting the giver. It also requires moral honesty: trust and courage in this story cannot be separated from violence that readers must neither conceal nor recruit for present-day harm.
