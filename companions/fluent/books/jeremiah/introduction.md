# Jeremiah book guide

## Begin Here

Jeremiah speaks through Judah's final decades before Jerusalem falls to Babylon. Called while young, he announces that temple, kingship, and land cannot shield a violent and unfaithful community. He is mocked, beaten, confined, and accused of betrayal, yet also speaks of return, a new covenant, and God's word written on the heart.

## At a Glance

- **Kind of writing:** Prophetic poetry, sermon, lament, sign-act, narrative, and letter.
- **Primary setting:** Judah, Jerusalem, and Egypt amid Babylonian expansion.
- **Central movement:** Persistent warning becomes catastrophe, yet judgment opens toward restoration and renewed covenant.
- **People to know:** Jeremiah, Baruch, Josiah, Jehoiakim, Zedekiah, priests, false prophets, and Babylonian officials.
- **Time to read aloud:** About 5 hours.

## What Is Going On?

The book is not arranged in a simple timeline. Oracles and stories circle recurring crises. Jeremiah's own complaints reveal the cost of carrying an unwelcome word; his anguish is part of his witness, not proof that faith removes distress.

## The Book's Movement

1. **Chapters 1–20:** Call, temple warning, symbolic acts, and personal laments.
2. **Chapters 21–29:** Kings, false hopes, Babylon's yoke, and a letter to exiles.
3. **Chapters 30–33:** A “book of consolation” promises return and new covenant.
4. **Chapters 34–45:** Jerusalem's last days, fall, aftermath, and flight to Egypt.
5. **Chapters 46–52:** Oracles concerning nations and a closing account of Jerusalem's fall.

## Watch For

- False peace versus truthful hope.
- Religious institutions held accountable for justice.
- The prophet arguing with God and his own vocation.
- Judgment that does not exhaust God's commitment.

## A Thread to Carry

Jeremiah shows that hope is not optimism protected from truth. Durable hope passes through grief, accountability, exile, and the promise that God can write faithfulness where external structures failed.
