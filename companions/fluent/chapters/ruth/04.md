# Ruth 4

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Boaz has promised Ruth that he will address her request, but a nearer relative has the first right to act. The story moves from the private threshing floor to the city gate, where legal and community matters are settled before witnesses.

### Chapter Path

- **4:1–6:** Boaz presents the land and Ruth together; the nearer redeemer declines.
- **4:7–12:** A sandal confirms the transaction; elders and people witness and bless Boaz and Ruth.
- **4:13–17:** Ruth bears Obed; Bethlehem's women celebrate Naomi's renewed life.
- **4:18–22:** A genealogy leads from Perez to David.

### Watch For

Notice who speaks for whom, who witnesses, and who receives blessing. Ruth becomes quieter in this chapter, so remember the courage and labor that brought the story here. Watch also how the women of Bethlehem interpret the child's birth in relation to Naomi.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How is the redemption settled? Why does the nearer relative decline? What does Obed's birth mean for Ruth, Naomi, and the future story?

### The Chapter in Brief

At the gate, Boaz gathers the nearer relative and ten elders. The relative initially agrees to redeem Naomi's land but withdraws when Ruth and the preservation of the deceased man's name become part of the responsibility. Boaz accepts the obligation before witnesses and marries Ruth. They have a son, Obed. The women bless God, celebrate Ruth's love and Naomi's restoration, and the genealogy identifies Obed as David's grandfather.

### Follow the Story

Redemption becomes tangible and named: land, household continuity, marriage, witnesses, and future care. Boaz does not rescue two helpless women by private generosity alone. He accepts a durable public commitment.

The nearer relative is not presented as a cartoon villain. He calculates that the obligation may endanger his own estate and declines. The contrast reveals that faithfulness can carry real cost.

The women say Ruth is better to Naomi than seven sons, an extraordinary affirmation in a culture that prized male heirs. Ruth the Moabite is not a side character in Israel's story. Her steadfast love becomes part of the line leading to David.

### Threads Through Scripture

The genealogy connects an intimate family story to Israel's royal history. The New Testament genealogy of Jesus also names Ruth, preserving the memory of a foreign woman whose loyalty and courage helped carry the promise forward. Redemption in Scripture repeatedly joins God's care with concrete human responsibility.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which commitments are made publicly, and why do witnesses matter?
- **Understand:** How does the chapter move from Naomi's “emptiness” in chapter 1 toward renewed fullness?
- **Connect:** What makes help durable rather than momentary?
- **Respond:** What shared, tangible, named commitment could strengthen a vulnerable person or community?

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

The legal relationship among land redemption, marriage, and preserving a deceased relative's name is complex. Ruth 4 resembles several Israelite practices without reproducing any one law in a simple way. The sandal functions as a public sign that a right has been transferred.

### Wrestle with This

Ancient negotiations often centered male household authority, and modern readers may rightly notice Ruth's limited voice at the gate. Read the chapter with both cultural honesty and memory of Ruth's earlier agency. The story commends a result that gives security and continuity, but it does not make every ancient social structure a timeless command.

### A Theme to Follow

The book's partnership develops from shared grief, to shared labor and protection, to a shared plan, and finally to public commitments that sustain a future.

### Further Study

Compare Ruth 4:13–17 with Matthew 1:1–6. Notice whose stories are carried into the genealogy and what that says about belonging.
