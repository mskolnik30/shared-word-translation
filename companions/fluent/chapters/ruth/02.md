# Ruth 2

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Naomi and Ruth have arrived in Bethlehem with little security. The barley harvest offers work through gleaning: gathering grain left behind after harvesters pass. Ruth asks to go to the fields, and the story brings her to land belonging to Boaz, a relative of Naomi's late husband.

### Chapter Path

- **2:1–7:** Ruth begins gleaning in Boaz's field.
- **2:8–16:** Boaz offers protection, water, food, and generous access to grain.
- **2:17–23:** Ruth returns with an abundant harvest; Naomi identifies Boaz as a family redeemer.

### Watch For

Notice how often Ruth is called “the Moabite.” Watch how Boaz's kindness becomes specific: instructions to workers, safe access, shared food, and extra grain. Good intentions become practices.

## Read the Chapter

Read Ruth 2 in the Fluent Translation. Track what Ruth, Boaz, the workers, and Naomi each contribute.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How does Ruth seek food? What has Boaz heard about her? What changes when Naomi learns whose field Ruth entered?

### The Chapter in Brief

Ruth goes out to glean and comes to Boaz's field. Boaz asks about her, learns of her steady work, and tells her to remain with his women workers. He orders that she be protected, gives her food and water, and makes sure extra grain is left for her. Ruth brings the grain to Naomi, who blesses Boaz and identifies him as one of their redeemers.

### Follow the Story

The narrator says Ruth “happened” to come to Boaz's field, allowing ordinary circumstance and providence to sit together. Ruth works hard, but her effort alone cannot make the field safe. Boaz has authority and uses it to change the conditions around her.

His blessing that Ruth has come under God's wings is immediately embodied in human action. Boaz does not pray for her protection and then leave her exposed. He instructs those under his authority and shares material resources.

### Threads Through Scripture

Israel's gleaning laws commanded landowners not to harvest every edge or retrieve every fallen stalk. The leftovers belonged to the poor, the immigrant, the widow, and the orphan. Ruth's experience shows how a law becomes life-giving when people practice more than minimum compliance.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which details show Ruth's initiative? Which show Boaz's responsibility?
- **Understand:** Why does protection from harassment matter as much as access to grain?
- **Connect:** Where can people technically be included but practically unsafe?
- **Respond:** What policy or habit could someone with power change to make belonging tangible?

### Prayer and Practice

Examine one space where you hold influence—a workplace, home, church, classroom, or group. Identify one concrete way to improve safety, access, or dignity for someone with less power.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Gleaning was not casual charity. It was a social practice built into Israel's law. The term “redeemer” describes a family member positioned to protect family land and continuity. Chapter 2 introduces the possibility without yet explaining how it will unfold.

### Wrestle with This

Ruth bows and asks why she has found favor as a foreigner. The scene can sound warm while revealing an unequal world in which her safety depends on a powerful man's conduct. The story commends Boaz because he recognizes and uses that power responsibly.

### A Theme to Follow

The Hebrew idea often translated steadfast kindness appears through loyalty that costs something. Trace how Ruth, Boaz, and Naomi each receive and extend such kindness.

### Further Study

Read Leviticus 19:9–10 and Deuteronomy 24:19–22. Compare the commands with the specific practices Boaz adds.
