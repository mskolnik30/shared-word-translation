# Ruth 1

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

During the period of Israel's judges, famine drives an Israelite family from Bethlehem to Moab. Elimelech dies, and later his sons Mahlon and Chilion die. Naomi, Ruth, and Orpah are left without husbands in a society where widowhood could bring severe economic danger.

### Chapter Path

- **1:1–5:** Famine, migration, marriage, and repeated loss.
- **1:6–14:** Naomi decides to return; she urges Ruth and Orpah to seek new lives in Moab.
- **1:15–18:** Ruth binds her future to Naomi, Naomi's people, and Naomi's God.
- **1:19–22:** Bethlehem receives them; Naomi names her bitterness and emptiness; barley harvest begins.

### Watch For

Notice the many uses of “return.” Attend to each woman's real options. Orpah's departure follows Naomi's instruction and should not be treated as betrayal. Ruth's decision is extraordinary because it accepts deep uncertainty.

## Read the Chapter

Read Ruth 1 in the Fluent Translation. Let the grief remain present even as the final line introduces harvest.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What has Naomi lost? What choices do Ruth and Orpah make? How does Naomi describe herself when she reaches Bethlehem?

### The Chapter in Brief

Famine takes Naomi's family to Moab, where her husband and two sons die. Hearing that food has returned to Judah, Naomi heads home and urges her daughters-in-law to remain in Moab. Orpah tearfully returns. Ruth refuses to leave Naomi and commits herself to her people and God. In Bethlehem, Naomi says she went away full and has returned empty. The two women arrive as the barley harvest begins.

### Follow the Story

Naomi's grief shapes the chapter. Her words about God's hand express how she understands her suffering; the narrator does not explain why each death occurred. Faithful reading can honor her honesty without turning every tragedy into a simple divine punishment.

Ruth's famous words are first a pledge of solidarity between two vulnerable women. They include faith, but they also involve place, people, death, and burial. Ruth does not offer comfort from a distance; she ties her own future to Naomi's.

### Threads Through Scripture

Famine and migration recall the stories of Abraham, Jacob, and Israel. Ruth's movement toward Bethlehem also joins a larger biblical pattern in which immigrants become central to God's work. Her commitment anticipates Scripture's repeated call to steadfast love expressed through presence and action.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Where does the word “return” carry physical, relational, or spiritual meaning?
- **Understand:** Why are the choices before Ruth, Orpah, and Naomi all costly?
- **Connect:** What is the difference between accompanying grief and trying to explain it?
- **Respond:** Who may need faithful presence more than quick advice?

### Prayer and Practice

Offer one concrete act of presence to someone carrying loss: a meal, a ride, a message without demands, or help with an ordinary task. Do not require them to make their grief sound hopeful.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Moab and Israel shared a difficult history, so Ruth's nationality would have been socially significant. Bethlehem means “house of bread,” making famine there especially striking. Naomi's proposed future through sons reflects ancient household structures, not a demand that modern widows remarry.

### Wrestle with This

Naomi asks to be called Mara, “bitter,” because she feels dealt with bitterly. Scripture preserves this protest inside the story. The harvest at chapter's end is a quiet opening, not a cancellation of her pain.

### Further Study

Compare Ruth's pledge with God's concern for immigrants and widows in Deuteronomy 10:17–19 and 24:17–22.
