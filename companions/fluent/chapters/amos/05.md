# Amos 5

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Amos has announced that Israel's privilege does not remove accountability. Chapter 5 is a funeral song for a nation still standing. It names corrupt courts, exactions from poor people, luxurious building, false worship, and misplaced desire for God's day.

### Chapter Path

- **5:1–9:** Lament announces collapse, yet “seek me and live” remains open.
- **5:10–15:** Truth is hated at the city gate; poor people are taxed while the rich build costly houses.
- **5:16–20:** Public mourning will spread; the day of the LORD will be darkness, not automatic victory.
- **5:21–27:** God rejects festivals and songs severed from justice and announces exile.

### Watch For

Seeking God, seeking good, and establishing justice belong together. The chapter is not anti-worship; it is against worship used to cover exploitation.

## Read the Chapter

Read Amos 5 in the Fluent Translation. Let “justice roll” receive its meaning from the specific economic and legal wrongs named around it.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

Why does Amos lament, what practices corrupt the gate, and why does God reject Israel's gatherings?

### The Chapter in Brief

Amos sings over fallen Israel and calls the people to seek the LORD rather than sanctuaries. The Creator who orders stars and day can bring destruction on strongholds. Israel hates correction, taxes poor people, accepts bribes, and denies needy people justice. Seeking good may open mercy for a remnant. Yet wailing approaches, and the day Israel desires will be darkness. God hates their festivals and songs because justice is absent and sends them toward exile.

### Follow the Story

The city gate functions as a court. When truthful witnesses are hated and bribes shape decisions, injustice becomes institutional. The prudent person's silence may be self-protection in a dangerous system, not praise for moral quietness.

The wealthy build stone houses and vineyards from extracted grain, but they will not enjoy them. Amos connects visible luxury with the neighbors who paid for it. “Perhaps” in verse 15 preserves restraint: reform is necessary, but mercy is not a lever humans pull.

The day of the LORD was expected to defeat Israel's enemies. Amos reverses that confidence. For an unjust covenant people, divine arrival exposes rather than endorses national identity. Justice must be like a stream that does not dry up seasonally—not an occasional project beside continuous exploitation.

### Threads Through Scripture

The Torah joins worship with fair courts and care for poor people. Isaiah similarly rejects assemblies accompanied by bloodshed. Jesus criticizes meticulous religion that neglects justice, mercy, and faithfulness.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What exact practices make justice bitter?
- **Understand:** Why does Amos overturn hope for the day of the LORD?
- **Connect:** What religious beauty can distract from material harm?
- **Respond:** Where can justice become continuous rather than occasional?

### Prayer and Practice

Trace one cost behind a product, institution, or convenience you enjoy. Support a change that increases fair hearing, wages, or access.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Pleiades and Orion are conventional identifications of Hebrew constellation names. The final cultic names are obscure; the main point is that worship objects will accompany Israel into exile.

### Wrestle with This

What would God refuse to hear in a community's worship because of what it refuses to hear outside worship?

### Further Study

Read Deuteronomy 16:18–20, Isaiah 1:10–20, and Matthew 23:23–24.
