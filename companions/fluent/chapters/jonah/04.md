# Jonah 4

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Nineveh has turned from violence, and God has relented from the announced disaster. What seems like prophetic success makes Jonah angry. For the first time, he explains why he fled: he expected that God's gracious character might lead to mercy.

### Chapter Path

- **4:1–4:** Jonah protests God's mercy and asks to die; God answers with a question.
- **4:5–8:** Jonah waits outside the city while God appoints a plant, a worm, and a scorching wind.
- **4:9–11:** Jonah grieves the plant; God compares Jonah's pity for it with God's concern for Nineveh and its animals.

### Watch For

Count the questions. The book ends without recording Jonah's answer. Notice how Jonah values the plant because it benefits him, while God names a whole city that cannot be reduced to Jonah's judgment.

## Read the Chapter

Read Jonah 4 in the Fluent Translation. Leave room for the final question to remain open.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

Why is Jonah angry? What does God appoint? What contrast does God draw at the end?

### The Chapter in Brief

Jonah is furious that Nineveh has received mercy. He recites Israel's confession that God is gracious, compassionate, slow to anger, and rich in steadfast love—but treats these qualities as a complaint. Outside the city, a plant gives him shade, a worm destroys it, and a hot wind overwhelms him. Jonah pities the plant. God asks whether God should not pity the great city, including many vulnerable people and animals.

### Follow the Story

Jonah knows God's character but struggles to desire what God desires. His anger is not hidden or silenced; God engages it with questions and a lived lesson. The plant exposes a compassion organized around personal comfort. Jonah grieves what served him but cannot yet grieve for people he regards as enemies.

The last question turns toward the reader. God's concern does not deny Nineveh's evil—the city was called to turn from violence. But judgment is not God's only relation to the city. God also sees its confusion, its many lives, and its animals.

### Threads Through Scripture

Jonah quotes the description of God's character revealed after Israel's own grave failure with the golden calf. Israel lives because God is merciful; Jonah resents that mercy when it reaches Nineveh. Jesus later commands love for enemies, carrying forward the difficult question of whether God's people will reflect God's generous concern.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What has God “appointed” across the book, and what does each appointed thing do?
- **Understand:** Why can a true statement about God become an accusation in Jonah's mouth?
- **Connect:** Whose pain is easiest for you to see? Whose humanity is hardest?
- **Respond:** How can a community seek both truthful accountability and the possibility of mercy?

### Prayer and Practice

Do not force yourself to feel instant affection for someone who has caused harm. Instead, pray for truth, protection, justice, and the refusal to erase anyone's humanity. If you are in danger, seek safety and wise help; this practice does not require renewed contact.

## Go Deeper

### Wrestle with This

Jonah's wish to die appears twice. The chapter does not praise despair or use God to shame a distressed person. God stays in conversation and asks questions. Readers experiencing thoughts of self-harm deserve immediate human care, not a moral lecture from this scene.

### A Theme to Follow

The book contrasts selective pity with God's wider compassion. Follow this theme through God's concern for the sailors, Jonah, Nineveh, those unable to discern, and the animals.

### More Than One Faithful Reading

“Those who do not know their right hand from their left” may refer to children, moral confusion, or the whole city's lack of discernment. Whichever emphasis is taken, the phrase deepens rather than narrows God's concern.

### Further Study

Read Exodus 34:6–7 beside Jonah 4:2. Ask why the same confession comforts Israel in one setting and angers Jonah in another.
