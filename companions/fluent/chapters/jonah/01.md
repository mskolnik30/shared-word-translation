# Jonah 1

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

The book opens without a long introduction. God tells Jonah to go east to Nineveh and speak against its evil. Jonah immediately travels west toward Tarshish. His flight carries him down—to Joppa, into a ship, into its hold, and finally into the sea.

### Chapter Path

- **1:1–3:** God's call and Jonah's flight.
- **1:4–10:** A violent storm, a sleeping prophet, and frightened sailors.
- **1:11–16:** Jonah is thrown into the sea; the storm stops; the sailors worship the LORD.
- **1:17:** God appoints a great fish to receive Jonah.

### Watch For

Notice how the foreign sailors act. They pray, ask questions, try to save Jonah, fear the LORD, and make vows. Jonah knows facts about God, yet he is the one fleeing. The chapter's irony invites reflection before it invites judgment.

## Read the Chapter

Read Jonah 1 in the Fluent Translation. Let the changing locations and repeated actions carry the story.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does God ask Jonah to do? How does Jonah respond? What do the sailors do before and after the sea becomes calm?

### The Chapter in Brief

God sends Jonah to Nineveh, but Jonah boards a ship for Tarshish. God sends a storm. The sailors discover that Jonah is fleeing from the LORD, yet they resist throwing him overboard until no other option remains. When they do, the sea becomes calm and they respond with reverence. God then appoints a great fish to keep Jonah alive.

### Follow the Story

Jonah's flight affects an entire ship. His private refusal becomes a shared danger. At the same time, people Jonah might regard as outsiders show courage and compassion. They do not treat Jonah as disposable. The chapter does not celebrate his plunge into the sea; it shows God pursuing a resistant prophet while awakening reverence among strangers.

The fish is introduced as God's provision, not Jonah's punishment alone. Jonah has reached the depths, but the story is not finished.

### Threads Through Scripture

God's call to speak against injustice recalls the work of Israel's prophets. The dangerous sea often represents forces beyond human control, while God's authority over it anticipates later biblical scenes of deliverance in storms. The sailors' growing fear of the LORD also fits a larger scriptural pattern in which outsiders sometimes recognize God's activity before insiders do.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which verbs of movement describe Jonah? Which describe God and the sailors?
- **Understand:** Why might Nineveh be a frightening or offensive destination for an Israelite prophet?
- **Connect:** When can one person's avoidance place strain on a whole community?
- **Respond:** What would honest, responsible obedience look like before a crisis grows?

### Prayer and Practice

Name one responsibility you have been avoiding. If it is safe and yours to carry, take one small step toward it. Pray for the humility to recognize wisdom and compassion in people outside your expected circle.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Tarshish probably represents a distant western destination; its exact location is debated. Nineveh was an important Assyrian city. The story's point does not depend on reconstructing Jonah's precise route: he is deliberately moving away from the place God names.

### Wrestle with This

Jonah tells the sailors to throw him into the sea, but the text does not explain every motive behind his words. Avoid turning this scene into a simple lesson about heroism or self-harm. The sailors first seek every possible way to reach land, and the chapter places life, responsibility, and mercy in tension.

### Further Study

Compare the sailors' questions in Jonah 1 with God's questions in Jonah 4. Ask what each set of questions reveals about responsibility and compassion.
