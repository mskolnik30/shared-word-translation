# Jonah 3

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Jonah is back on dry land. God speaks a second time and gives essentially the same commission: go to Nineveh and announce the message God gives. This time Jonah goes.

### Chapter Path

- **3:1–4:** Jonah receives the call again and enters Nineveh with a warning.
- **3:5–9:** The people believe God; fasting and mourning spread from ordinary residents to the king and even to the treatment of animals.
- **3:10:** God sees their changed conduct and relents from the announced disaster.

### Watch For

Notice the speed and breadth of Nineveh's response. The text emphasizes that God sees what the people do—their turning from evil and violence—not only what they say or wear.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does Jonah announce? How do the people and king respond? What does God see?

### The Chapter in Brief

God renews Jonah's call, and Jonah goes to Nineveh. He announces that the city will be overturned in forty days. The people believe God and begin a citywide fast. The king steps down, joins the mourning, commands people to turn from violence, and expresses hope that God may relent. God sees their changed actions and does not bring the announced disaster.

### Follow the Story

The chapter shows the purpose of prophetic warning: not simply to predict destruction, but to call people away from destructive conduct. The king does not claim entitlement to mercy. “Who knows?” expresses hope without control. Repentance is concrete because violence must cease.

The Hebrew word behind “overturned” can carry more than one possibility: a city may be destroyed, or it may be profoundly changed. Nineveh is, at least within this scene, overturned through repentance rather than ruined by disaster.

### Threads Through Scripture

Biblical prophets often announce judgment so that hearers may turn and live. Jeremiah 18 describes God responding to the conduct of nations, while Joel uses fasting and the hopeful question of whether God may relent. Jesus later refers to the people of Nineveh as people who responded to Jonah's proclamation.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What details show that repentance includes the whole city?
- **Understand:** Why does the chapter emphasize turning from violence?
- **Connect:** What is the difference between public remorse and changed practice?
- **Respond:** Is there a harmful pattern your community can name and begin to change together?

### Prayer and Practice

Pray for a community harmed by violence or exploitation. Choose one concrete action that supports repair, safety, or truth rather than stopping at general concern.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

The book presents Nineveh as exceedingly great, using scale to heighten the drama. Ancient population figures, city boundaries, and the story's date remain debated. The theological force is clear without forcing the narrative into a modern census or map.

### More Than One Faithful Reading

Readers differ on how the account relates to specific moments in Assyrian history. The chapter itself centers on a moral and theological claim: God attends to human violence, hears repentance, and is free to relent.

### Wrestle with This

The story does not ask readers to minimize Assyria's cruelty or trust unsafe people without evidence. It depicts repentance as a turn from evil and violence. Mercy and accountability are not opposites here.

### Further Study

Compare Jonah 3:10 with Jeremiah 18:7–10. Observe how both passages connect warning, changed conduct, and God's response.
