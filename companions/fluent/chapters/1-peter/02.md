# 1 Peter 2

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Believers have been given new birth through God's enduring word and called to sincere family love. Chapter 2 moves from communal identity as living stones and holy people to public conduct under authorities and unjust suffering inside slavery. Its submission language must not be used to protect abuse or make slavery good.

### Chapter Path

- **2:1–3:** Put away relational corruption and desire nourishing word.
- **2:4–10:** Coming to Christ the living stone, believers become a spiritual house, priestly people, and recipients of mercy.
- **2:11–17:** As sojourners, practice honorable conduct, submit for the Lord's sake, live free as God's servants, honor everyone.
- **2:18–25:** Enslaved household members suffering unjustly are addressed through Christ's non-retaliating suffering and healing.

### Watch For

Individual stones become a shared structure. Identity is corporate before the chapter addresses public witness.

## Read the Chapter

Read 1 Peter 2 in the Fluent Translation. If submission or unjust suffering has been used against you, read with support and keep the difference between survival counsel and moral approval clear.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

Who are the believers together, and how does the chapter ask them to live under suspicion and unequal power?

### The Chapter in Brief

Believers put away malice, deceit, hypocrisy, envy, and slander and long for nourishment. Coming to rejected but chosen Christ, they become living stones, a holy priesthood, and a people called from darkness into light and mercy. As outsiders, their honorable conduct may become witness. They submit to institutions for the Lord's sake while living as free servants of God and honoring everyone. Enslaved household members are told that unjust suffering may follow Christ's example: he did not retaliate, entrusted himself to just judgment, bore sins, and returns straying people to the Shepherd.

### Follow the Story

Israel's vocation language is applied to the church to name mercy and mission, not to authorize contempt for Jewish people. The stone quotations hold assurance and stumbling without explaining every aspect of divine purpose and human response.

Submission is “for the Lord's sake,” so human authority is not absolute. “Honor everyone” prevents selective dignity, while fear belongs to God rather than emperor. Christians have used the slave instructions to defend slavery and force victims to endure abuse. That misuse must be rejected. The text speaks to people with little legal power about preserving agency and witness; it does not command slaveholding or forbid escape, protection, protest, or abolition.

Christ's non-retaliation is grounded in entrusting justice to God, not pretending wrong is right. His suffering is redemptive in a way no victim is required to reproduce for an abuser.

### Threads Through Scripture

The living-stone texts come from Psalms and Isaiah. Exodus calls Israel a priestly kingdom. Jesus rejects retaliation while confronting wrongdoing, and Peter later forbids elders to dominate.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which identities are plural and shared?
- **Understand:** What limits human submission?
- **Connect:** How has suffering language protected people in power?
- **Respond:** How can your community protect someone with less legal or social power?

### Prayer and Practice

Honor one person whose social role makes them easy to overlook. Strengthen a concrete safety or advocacy practice; do not counsel passive endurance of abuse.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Household slavery differed in form across the Roman world but remained ownership and coercion. “Servants” here are enslaved household members, not modern employees.

### Wrestle with This

How can non-retaliation resist the formation of hatred without surrendering the pursuit of safety and justice?

### Further Study

Read Exodus 19:3–6, Isaiah 53, Acts 5:27–32, and 1 Peter 5:1–5.
