# Nahum 1

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Nahum speaks about Nineveh, capital of the Assyrian Empire, from the horizon of people who know Assyrian conquest and terror. The opening poem holds together God's patience, power, judgment, goodness, and protection of those seeking refuge.

### Chapter Path

- **1:1–6:** The LORD is jealous and avenging, slow to anger yet unwilling to clear guilt; creation trembles before divine power.
- **1:7–8:** The LORD is good and knows those taking refuge, while an overwhelming flood pursues enemies.
- **1:9–14:** Plots, oppressive strength, and idolatrous continuity come to an end; Judah's yoke is broken.
- **1:15:** A messenger crosses the mountains announcing peace and renewed worship.

### Watch For

Verse 7 stands inside the judgment poem, not outside it. God's goodness is experienced as refuge by those under threat and as the end of an order that continually harms them.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does the poem say about God's character, Assyria's power, and Judah's future?

### The Chapter in Brief

Nahum announces an oracle concerning Nineveh. The LORD's jealous commitment and vengeance are set beside patience and great power. Sea, rivers, fertile regions, mountains, and earth respond to God's arrival. No one can stand before divine indignation, yet the LORD is good, a stronghold who knows those taking refuge. Nineveh's plotting and strength will not survive. God promises afflicted Judah that the oppressor's yoke and bonds will be broken. The chapter ends with good news crossing the mountains: peace is proclaimed and Judah may again celebrate its festivals.

### Follow the Story

Nahum uses vengeance language that can sound dangerous when placed in human hands. In the poem, vengeance belongs to God; it is not delegated as private retaliation. The prophetic hope is that an empire capable of repeated cruelty will not remain unaccountable forever.

The line “slow to anger” echoes Israel's confession of God's mercy, but here patience does not mean indifference to victims. Neither patience nor judgment cancels the other. The text resists a choice between a God who cares for the harmed and a God who is merciful.

Images of storm, drought, earthquake, flood, fire, and broken rock express the reach of divine presence. They should not be used to identify particular natural disasters as punishment. Nahum's poetry announces the collapse of Assyrian security, not a technique for decoding later catastrophes.

### Threads Through Scripture

The description “slow to anger” recalls Exodus 34 and Jonah 4. Isaiah also pictures good news coming over mountains, and Paul later reuses that messenger image when speaking about proclamation.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which descriptions of God appear side by side?
- **Understand:** How can the same divine action be refuge for some and judgment for others?
- **Connect:** What is lost when patience is confused with permission for abuse to continue?
- **Respond:** Where can you support safety and accountable justice without seeking personal revenge?

### Prayer and Practice

Pray for people living under organized violence. Support one trustworthy effort that protects victims, documents wrongdoing, or provides refuge.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Assyria used forced displacement and public terror to control conquered peoples. Nahum's fierce hope emerges from that history. Verse 15 is Nahum 2:1 in the Masoretic chapter division but remains 1:15 in common English numbering.

### Wrestle with This

How can communities name the need for judgment without imitating the cruelty they oppose?
