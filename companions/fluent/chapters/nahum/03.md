# Nahum 3

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Nahum announces Nineveh's fall from the perspective of peoples repeatedly harmed by Assyrian violence. Chapter 3 includes battlefield death, sexualized humiliation, slaughtered infants in remembered conquest, and a gendered military taunt. These images expose empire but are not models for how readers should speak about women, enemies, or victims.

### Chapter Path

- **3:1–7:** The “city of blood” faces battle and public exposure for violence, deception, and exploitation.
- **3:8–11:** Fallen Thebes proves that water and international allies cannot make a city invulnerable.
- **3:12–17:** Nineveh's defenses, soldiers, merchants, and officials collapse through images of figs, fire, and locusts.
- **3:18–19:** Leaders are still, people scatter, and nations applaud because Assyria's cruelty touched them continually.

### Watch For

The final applause is not random cruelty; it reveals the breadth of Assyria's victims. Still, the poem's own humiliating language should not be repeated as permission to dehumanize.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

Why is Nineveh called a city of blood, and what examples and images dismantle its claim to permanence?

### The Chapter in Brief

Nahum pronounces woe on Nineveh, a city filled with blood, lies, and plunder. Rapid battle sounds lead into piles of bodies. Nineveh's exploitative political relationships are pictured as prostitution and sorcery, followed by threatened exposure. The prophet points to Thebes, whose waters and allies did not prevent conquest. Nineveh's defenses will fall like ripe figs; its officials will disappear like locusts. Its shepherds no longer gather the people, and nations clap at the fall of a power whose evil repeatedly reached them.

### Follow the Story

Assyria used terror strategically. Nahum reverses that public spectacle against the empire: the one that exposed nations will be exposed. The reversal speaks hope to victims but uses the female body as a site of humiliation. Responsible readers should name that feature rather than treating sexualized violence as divine rhetoric to imitate.

Thebes had waterways, walls, and international support, yet Assyria itself conquered it. Nineveh is asked whether it is better. Empires forget that the vulnerability they exploited is also their own.

Locusts picture both multiplication and disappearance. Merchants and officials flourish while conditions serve them, then fly away as the system collapses. Leadership that organized extraction cannot shepherd people through ruin.

### Threads Through Scripture

Jonah earlier portrays Nineveh receiving mercy, while Nahum speaks to a later horizon of renewed Assyrian violence. Together they resist both denying mercy and denying accountability. Revelation similarly announces the fall of an exploitative imperial city.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which images reverse Assyria's own practices?
- **Understand:** Why do the nations celebrate Nineveh's end?
- **Connect:** How can victims seek accountability without absorbing an oppressor's dehumanization?
- **Respond:** What truth about an abusive system needs public memory?

### Prayer and Practice

Pray for oppressed communities awaiting an end to violence. Practice speaking about accountability without sexual humiliation, gender contempt, or delight in suffering.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

No-Amon is Thebes in Egypt. The military taunt comparing soldiers to women reflects ancient gender stereotypes retained from the source, not endorsed as truth.

### Wrestle with This

How can Scripture give victims fierce hope while readers critically refuse rhetoric that harms another vulnerable group?

Read Jonah 3–4, Isaiah 10:5–19, and Revelation 18.
