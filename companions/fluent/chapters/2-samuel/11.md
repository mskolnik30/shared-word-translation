# 2 Samuel 11

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

David's kingdom is secure, and chapter 10 ended with victory over powerful enemies. Chapter 11 turns from public success to royal abuse. It contains sexual exploitation, pregnancy under coercive power, deception, murder, and collateral death. David is the responsible ruler. Bathsheba should not be blamed or described as a willing partner in an “affair.”

### Chapter Path

- **11:1–5:** David remains in Jerusalem, sees Bathsheba, sends for her, takes her, and learns she is pregnant.
- **11:6–13:** David summons Uriah and tries to make him go home; Uriah refuses ordinary comfort while soldiers remain exposed.
- **11:14–17:** David sends a death order by Uriah's own hand; Joab arranges a battle position that kills Uriah and others.
- **11:18–25:** Joab reports; David treats the deaths as ordinary fortunes of war.
- **11:26–27:** Bathsheba mourns; David takes her into his house; the narrator names his action evil before the LORD.

### Watch For

Follow the verbs of royal power: David sends, asks, takes, summons, writes, and commands. Notice how Uriah's loyalty contrasts with David's effort to protect himself.

## Read the Chapter

Read 2 Samuel 11 in the Fluent Translation. If abuse of power or sexual harm is close to your experience, read with support or stop when needed.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

Without adding motives the text does not give, trace how David uses authority to create harm and then tries to conceal it.

### The Chapter in Brief

While his army is at war, David sees Bathsheba bathing. After learning that she is Uriah's wife, he sends messengers, takes her, and sleeps with her. She becomes pregnant. David summons Uriah, hoping he will sleep at home and hide the child's paternity. Uriah refuses comfort out of solidarity with the army. David then orders Joab to place him in deadly combat. Uriah and other soldiers die. Bathsheba mourns; David takes her as his wife. The chapter concludes that David's action was evil to God.

### Follow the Story

The opening sentence is an ethical signal: at the time kings go out to battle, David stays behind. The narrator then piles up acts of command. Bathsheba is identified through family relationships and summoned by the king. In an absolute monarchy, she has no meaningful power to refuse. The text gives no basis for imagining seduction on her part.

Uriah, a foreign-born soldier, practices the solidarity David has abandoned. He will not enjoy his home while the ark, Israel, Judah, and fellow soldiers remain in temporary shelters. David first manipulates his loyalty, then weaponizes it: Uriah unknowingly carries the order for his own death.

The plan also kills unnamed soldiers. David's attempt to hide one abuse widens the circle of harm. His message that “the sword devours one as well as another” disguises targeted murder as impersonal wartime loss. Power controls not only bodies but also the story told about their deaths.

Bathsheba is still called “the wife of Uriah” after David takes her into his house. The wording refuses David's attempt to make the situation respectable. The final moral judgment is brief and clear.

### Threads Through Scripture

Israel's law warned that kings could take people and property. Nathan's confrontation in the next chapter interprets David's conduct through a story about a rich man taking a poor man's beloved lamb. Prophets continue to expose rulers who rename seizure and violence as lawful power. Jesus describes leadership as service rather than domination.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which commands allow David to remain physically distant from the harm he causes?
- **Understand:** Why is “affair” an inaccurate description of this event?
- **Connect:** How do institutions turn deliberate harm into neutral-sounding procedure?
- **Respond:** What accountability helps keep entrusted power from becoming private possession?

### Prayer and Practice

Pray for survivors of sexual exploitation and for people who confront powerful offenders. Examine one area where you have authority. Invite a trustworthy person to name effects you may not see. If you are experiencing abuse, prioritize safety and qualified help; forgiveness does not require continued access.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Bathsheba's bathing follows purification from menstruation, which makes the timing of pregnancy clear; it is not portrayed as an attempt to attract David. The Hebrew verb “take” is common but becomes ominous in a chapter dominated by royal power.

### Wrestle with This

The Bible preserves both David's covenant importance and a devastating account of his abuse. What becomes possible when a community refuses to protect a leader's reputation at the cost of truth?

### Further Study

Read 1 Samuel 8:10–18, 2 Samuel 12:1–14, and Psalm 51. Keep confession, consequence, and the voices of those harmed distinct.
