# Jude 1

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Jude writes to people who are called, loved, and kept. Corrupt teachers have entered their shared life and turned grace into permission for exploitation while denying Jesus' authority. Jude responds with remembered judgments, vivid metaphors, practical exhortation, and a final doxology.

### Chapter Path

- **1:1–4:** Identity, blessing, and the need to contend for the received faith.
- **1:5–16:** Stories of judgment and accumulating images expose the teachers' conduct.
- **1:17–23:** Remember the apostles; build up the community; remain in love; show discerning mercy.
- **1:24–25:** Praise to the God able to keep and present people with joy.

### Watch For

Jude's opponents are described by their effects: sensual self-interest, rejection of authority, slander, greed, self-feeding leadership, empty promise, favoritism, and division.

## Read the Chapter

Read Jude in the Fluent Translation. Let unfamiliar references remain unfamiliar long enough to observe what Jude does with them.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

Why does Jude change his planned subject? What patterns does he condemn? What practices does he give the beloved community?

### The Chapter in Brief

Jude urges believers to contend for the faith because ungodly people have twisted grace and denied Jesus' rule. He recalls Israel's unbelief, rebellious angels, Sodom and Gomorrah, Cain, Balaam, and Korah, then describes the teachers as hidden reefs, self-feeding shepherds, waterless clouds, fruitless trees, wild waves, and wandering stars. By contrast, the beloved community should remember apostolic warnings, build itself up in faith, pray in the Spirit, remain in God's love, wait for Christ's mercy, and offer differentiated mercy to people in doubt or danger. Jude closes by praising God, who is able to keep them and present them with joy.

### Follow the Story

The letter's harshest words target leaders who participate in the community's love feasts while feeding themselves. Their theology and conduct belong together: grace has been changed into cover for self-serving desire.

Even Michael's example restrains arrogant speech. He does not claim authority to pronounce his own reviling judgment but leaves rebuke to the Lord. “Contend” therefore cannot mean imitating the slander Jude condemns.

Verses 22–23 call for different responses rather than a single aggressive method. Doubters receive mercy. People in danger need urgent help. Helpers must remain alert to corruption. The exact wording varies among ancient manuscripts, but discerning mercy remains central.

### Threads Through Scripture

Jude gathers Israel's wilderness story, Genesis, prophetic warnings, and remembered rebels. He also draws on Jewish works such as 1 Enoch and a tradition about Moses' body. His closing praise echoes the broad biblical confession that preservation and final joy depend on God's action.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which metaphors emphasize danger, emptiness, instability, or failed leadership?
- **Understand:** How do verses 20–23 describe faithful contending?
- **Connect:** When can grace-language be used to excuse exploitation or avoid accountability?
- **Respond:** Who needs patient mercy, who needs urgent protection, and what boundaries keep help safe?

### Prayer and Practice

Pray the closing doxology for your community. Then choose one practice from verses 20–23: strengthen shared faith, pray, remain in love, wait with hope, show mercy, or help someone leave danger. Do not attempt a dangerous intervention alone.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Jude's citation of Enoch and reference to Michael show that biblical writers could use familiar Jewish traditions to make a theological point. Citation does not require readers to treat every part of those works as Scripture.

### Wrestle with This

Sodom has often been reduced to a slogan in modern debates. Jude emphasizes transgressive desire within a chain of rebellion and exploitation. Genesis and the prophets also associate Sodom with attempted sexual violence, arrogance, excess, and neglect of the poor. The reference should not be used to target vulnerable people.

### Further Study

Compare Jude with 2 Peter 2. Identify shared images and notice how Jude's warning is enclosed by God's keeping love.
