### Follow the Movement

The order of Ezra 10 matters. Its 7 named sections move from “There Is Still Hope for Israel” through “The Cases Are Examined” to “Men of Israel.” Read that complete arc before using one detail to characterize the chapter as a whole.

The translation note at verse 2 observes: “foreign women” identifies wives from the surrounding peoples in the specific post-exilic covenant crisis described in Ezra 9–10. The text’s concern is covenant identity and practice; it should not be converted into a modern racial category. The translation note at verse 3 observes: The proposal to send away wives and children comes from Shecaniah and the gathered community; Ezra 9 records no direct divine command to divorce these specific marriages. The community understands its proposal as applying the instruction, but the narrative’s distinction between divine command, Ezra’s prayer, and Shecaniah’s proposed remedy should be retained. One vocabulary entry at verse 1 identifies יָדָה (yadah) “To confess” or “give thanks.” Here Ezra openly acknowledges communal sin before God.
