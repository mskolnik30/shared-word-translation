# Habakkuk 3

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Habakkuk has protested violence in Judah and the rise of a still more violent Babylon. After taking his watchpost and hearing that arrogant empire will not endure, he closes with a prayer shaped like a song. Fear remains present all the way to the final confession of trust.

### Chapter Path

- **3:1–2:** Habakkuk asks God to renew divine work and remember compassion within wrath.
- **3:3–7:** God comes from the south in splendor; plague, nations, mountains, and tents react.
- **3:8–15:** Divine-warrior imagery recalls victory over waters and enemies for the salvation of God's people.
- **3:16–19:** Habakkuk trembles, waits for distress, imagines total agricultural failure, and nevertheless rejoices in God.

### Watch For

The chapter never says that faith removes bodily fear or makes scarcity pleasant. Habakkuk trembles and trusts in the same prayer.

## Read the Chapter

Read Habakkuk 3 in the Fluent Translation. Notice the repeated bodily and creation-wide responses to God's coming.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does Habakkuk ask God to remember, what older deliverance does the poem evoke, and what remains absent when he rejoices?

### The Chapter in Brief

Habakkuk asks God to revive divine work and remember compassion within wrath. A poetic vision portrays God coming from Teman and Paran with brightness, plague, trembling nations, shattered mountains, surging waters, and cosmic weapons. The divine warrior goes out to save the people and the anointed, overturning a wicked house and enemies who would devour the poor. Hearing this, Habakkuk's body trembles and decays with fear, yet he waits quietly for the invader's day of distress. Even if figs, grapes, olives, fields, sheep, and cattle all fail, he will rejoice in the God of salvation, who gives him sure-footed strength.

### Follow the Movement

The prayer looks backward in order to face forward. Images of Sinai, exodus, sea, sun, moon, and divine battle gather memories of God's saving action without forming a simple historical replay. Several lines remain deliberately difficult, and the Companion should not identify every image more narrowly than the poetry does.

“In wrath, remember compassion” does not deny judgment. It asks that judgment never become the whole truth about God's work. The request becomes especially important because the poem itself uses overwhelming images of plague, shattered creation, and crushed enemies.

The final confession begins after a complete inventory of loss. It should not be imposed on grieving people as compulsory cheerfulness or proof of sufficient faith. Habakkuk speaks his own trust while his body still trembles. Communities can receive that witness without demanding that someone else reach the same words on schedule.

### Threads Through Scripture

The southern divine appearance recalls traditions around Sinai. The sea imagery echoes exodus deliverance, while the failing fig tree later appears in prophetic and Gospel warnings. The deer's secure footing resembles language in Psalm 18.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which parts of Habakkuk's body respond to what he hears?
- **Understand:** Why does he ask for compassion within wrath?
- **Connect:** How can remembered deliverance sustain people without erasing present fear?
- **Respond:** What form of trust is possible for you without pretending that loss is good?

### Prayer and Practice

Write two honest lines: “Though…” naming a real loss, and “yet…” naming the smallest truthful ground for continued trust. Leave the second line blank if it cannot yet be spoken.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Shigionoth and Selah are retained musical or liturgical terms whose precise functions are uncertain. “Your anointed” may refer to a king, the people, or another chosen representative; the text does not settle it.

### Wrestle with This

How can a community honor testimony of resilient joy without using it to rush another person's grief?

### Further Study

Read Exodus 15, Psalm 18:25–36, and Habakkuk 1:12–2:4.
