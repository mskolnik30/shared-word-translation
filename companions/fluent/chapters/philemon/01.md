# Philemon 1

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Paul is imprisoned and writes to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the church meeting in their house. The subject is Onesimus, an enslaved man whose relationship with both Paul and Philemon is being transformed.

### Chapter Path

- **1:1–7:** Paul gives thanks for Philemon's love and prays that shared trust becomes effective.
- **1:8–14:** Paul could command but appeals in love; Onesimus has become his child and his heart.
- **1:15–21:** Receive Onesimus as a beloved brother and as Paul; charge any debt to Paul.
- **1:22–25:** Paul hopes to visit and closes with greetings.

### Watch For

Track the words “love,” “partner,” “heart,” and “receive.” Ask how Paul's appeal places social and economic weight on the theological claim that Onesimus is a brother.

## Read the Chapter

Read Philemon in the Fluent Translation. Remember that the house church hears a letter about a relationship structured by slavery.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does Paul ask Philemon to do? What costs does Paul offer to carry? What remains unstated?

### The Chapter in Brief

Paul thanks God for Philemon's love and for the way he refreshes believers. He then appeals for Onesimus, whom he calls his child and his own heart. Paul sends him back rather than acting without Philemon's consent, but he reframes the relationship: Onesimus is no longer merely a slave but a beloved brother. If Philemon regards Paul as a partner, he should welcome Onesimus as Paul himself. Paul offers to repay any debt and expresses confidence that Philemon will do more than requested.

### Follow the Story

Paul uses tenderness and social pressure together. He names his age and chains, addresses the church, reminds Philemon of debt, and anticipates a visit. The appeal is not free of power. Yet Paul directs his influence toward Onesimus's welcome and offers to absorb material loss.

“Perhaps” in verse 15 matters. Paul proposes a providential possibility without claiming certainty about why separation occurred.

The phrase “no longer as a slave” changes the moral imagination of the relationship. Many interpreters see manumission as the fitting “more” Paul expects, while others note that the letter never states the legal outcome. That tension should remain visible.

### Threads Through Scripture

Israel's story remembers liberation from slavery and commands protection for vulnerable workers and fugitives. In Paul's letters, baptismal belonging challenges status divisions within Christ's body. Philemon presses that claim into a household where one believer legally owns another.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which requests are direct, and which expectations are implied?
- **Understand:** Why is welcome “as you would me” socially and materially demanding?
- **Connect:** Where do communities use family language while preserving harmful status differences?
- **Respond:** What relationship needs more than kind feeling—perhaps changed policy, restitution, or relinquished control?

### Prayer and Practice

Identify one benefit you receive from another person's constrained or poorly rewarded labor. Take one concrete step toward fair pay, voice, credit, or safer conditions.

## Go Deeper

### Wrestle with This

The letter has been used both to challenge slavery and to avoid directly abolishing it. Modern readers should not soften the word *slave* into employee or treat ancient slavery as harmless. Onesimus's humanity and freedom must not disappear behind admiration for Paul's diplomacy.

### More Than One Faithful Reading

We do not know whether Onesimus fled, was sent, sought mediation, or encountered Paul another way. Nor do we know the exact debt. Responsible interpretation distinguishes the letter's words from later reconstructions.

### Further Study

Read Galatians 3:26–29 and Colossians 4:7–9. Ask what shared identity in Christ requires within unequal social systems.
