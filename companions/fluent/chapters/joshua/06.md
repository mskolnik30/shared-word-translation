# Joshua 6

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Israel has crossed the Jordan, raised a memorial, renewed circumcision and Passover, and encountered the commander of the LORD's army. Jericho is the first fortified city in the land. This chapter combines ritual procession, divine gift, rescue, and total destruction. It should be read without hiding its violence or turning it into a modern battle plan.

### Chapter Path

- **6:1–7:** God gives Joshua unusual instructions for circling the closed city.
- **6:8–14:** Priests, ark, armed guards, and people march in silence for six days.
- **6:15–21:** On the seventh day the people shout, the wall falls, and the city is placed under destruction.
- **6:22–25:** Rahab and her household are brought out and preserved.
- **6:26–27:** Joshua pronounces a curse against rebuilding the city's fortifications.

### Watch For

The ark and priests remain central; Israel does not win by ordinary siegecraft. Also hold together what the story emphasizes and what it leaves unheard: Rahab is rescued, while the other inhabitants are not given voices.

## Read the Chapter

Read Joshua 6 in the Fluent Translation. Do not hurry past verse 21. Let the moral difficulty remain part of the reading.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How does Israel approach Jericho? What happens to the city, and why is Rahab's household treated differently?

### The Chapter in Brief

Jericho is sealed against Israel. God instructs the army, priests, and ark to circle the city for seven days. On the seventh day, after seven circuits, the people shout and the wall collapses. Israel destroys the city's inhabitants and sets apart its metal for the sanctuary treasury. The spies rescue Rahab and her family because she protected them. Joshua curses anyone who rebuilds Jericho's fortifications.

### Follow the Story

The slow procession makes the event look like worship rather than military technique. Silence, trumpets, the ark, and the repeated number seven place the outcome under God's action. Israel receives the city; it cannot claim mastery through superior weapons.

The city is placed under *herem*, a severe form of devotion to destruction. The chapter's language is comprehensive and includes civilians and animals. Faithful readers should neither pretend the violence is absent nor assume that every ancient war summary answers all historical questions. The next chapter will show that Israel also falls under judgment when Achan takes what was devoted; ethnic identity does not place Israel beyond accountability.

Rahab's rescue matters. She is a Canaanite woman whose trust and protection of the messengers bring her household into Israel. Yet her inclusion does not remove the deaths around her. The story provides no speeches from the other people inside Jericho, and responsible reading should acknowledge that silence.

### Threads Through Scripture

The seven-day pattern recalls creation and Sabbath, while the ark recalls God's wilderness presence. Rahab later appears in Jesus' genealogy and among examples of active faith. Biblical prophets will also condemn Israel's own injustice and announce loss of land, showing that possession is never unconditional permission.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which details make the march a liturgical act rather than a normal siege?
- **Understand:** What does Rahab's rescue complicate in the story?
- **Connect:** How have conquest stories been misused to justify displacement, colonialism, or war?
- **Respond:** What practice helps you remain honest before a text you cannot make morally easy?

### Prayer and Practice

Lament before God for civilians harmed by war, ancient and present. Refuse language that turns whole peoples into obstacles or treats violence as spiritually thrilling.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Ancient victory accounts often use sweeping totality language even when later texts describe inhabitants remaining in the land. Joshua itself combines broad conquest summaries with unfinished occupation. That literary pattern may inform historical questions, but it does not make the narrated violence morally weightless.

### Wrestle with This

What does it mean to receive this chapter as Scripture while refusing to recruit it as authorization for violence against present-day communities?

### Further Study

Read Joshua 2 for Rahab's decision, Joshua 7 for Israel's own accountability, and Amos 1–2 for judgment falling on several nations—including Israel—for violence.
