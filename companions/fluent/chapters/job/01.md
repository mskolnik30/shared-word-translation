# Job 1

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Job opens by telling readers something his friends will later deny: Job is blameless and upright. The chapter contains sudden loss of livelihood, servants, and all Job's children. If you are carrying fresh grief, read gently and reject any claim that tragedy proves hidden guilt.

### Chapter Path

- **1:1–5:** Job's integrity, household, wealth, and regular prayers are described.
- **1:6–12:** In the heavenly council, the accuser questions whether Job reveres God only because he is protected.
- **1:13–19:** Four messengers report cascading theft, fire, violence, and the deaths of Job's children.
- **1:20–22:** Job mourns, worships, and does not charge God with wrongdoing.

### Watch For

Readers see a heavenly conversation Job never hears. That scene frames the book's question but does not give Job an explanation he can use.

## Read the Chapter

Read Job 1 in the Fluent Translation. Pause between the messengers; the narrative's speed is part of its devastation.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does the narrator establish about Job? What question does the accuser raise, and how does Job first respond to catastrophe?

### The Chapter in Brief

Job is introduced as a person of integrity who reveres God and turns from evil. He has a large family and great wealth and regularly offers sacrifices for his children. In God's court, the accuser argues that Job's reverence is purchased by protection. Permission is given to strike what Job has, though not Job himself. Raiders, fire, and wind destroy his possessions, servants, and children in rapid succession. Job tears his robe, shaves his head, bows, and worships. The narrator says he does not sin in this response.

### Follow the Story

“Blameless” means integrity, not sinless perfection. The description establishes a boundary: the losses are not evidence that Job secretly deserved them. Any later speech that assumes suffering proves guilt contradicts what readers already know.

The accuser asks whether Job reveres God “for nothing”—freely, without payment. That question reaches beyond Job: is relationship with God only an exchange for safety and prosperity? The test is horrifying because Job's family and servants suffer within it. The book does not ask readers to call their deaths small or merely instructional.

Job's first words are often quoted as calm acceptance, but his body is already lamenting. Torn clothing, shaved head, and falling to the ground are practices of extreme grief. Worship and devastation occupy the same moment. His response is not the only faithful form grief may take; chapter 3 will give him words of raw protest.

### Threads Through Scripture

Job challenges simplistic readings of covenant blessing and suffering. The Psalms likewise preserve innocent lament and accusations addressed to God. Jesus rejects the assumption that particular tragedies prove greater sin and weeps at a friend's grave.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What does the narrator tell you before suffering begins?
- **Understand:** Why is the accuser's “for nothing” question central?
- **Connect:** Which explanations offered to grieving people protect certainty more than they offer care?
- **Respond:** How can you accompany pain without explaining it?

### Prayer and Practice

Offer quiet presence to someone grieving. Do not search for a lesson in their loss. If the grief is yours, use as few or as many words as you have.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

“The accuser” describes a role in the heavenly council; Hebrew includes the definite article rather than using Satan as a simple personal name. Job's sacrifices express attentive care, not evidence that his children had sinned.

### Wrestle with This

How do you read a story in which God permits devastating testing without turning mystery into an excuse for careless claims about real suffering?

### Further Study

Read Job 2–3, Psalm 13, and Luke 13:1–5, noticing different faithful responses to suffering.
