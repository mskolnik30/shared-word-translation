# Micah 6

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Micah has condemned leaders who seize land, devour people, and build Zion with blood, while also promising gathered survivors and peace. Chapter 6 stages a covenant lawsuit. Mountains hear God's case, saving history is recalled, anxious worship escalates, and ordinary commerce is examined.

### Chapter Path

- **6:1–5:** Creation witnesses as God asks how the people experienced covenant as a burden and recalls deliverance.
- **6:6–8:** A worshiper imagines increasingly extreme offerings; the answer names justice, faithful kindness, and humble walking.
- **6:9–12:** The city is called to account for dishonest measures, violence, and deceit.
- **6:13–16:** Labor without fulfillment follows a legacy shaped by Omri and Ahab.

### Watch For

Verse 8 is not an abstract motto detached from the chapter. Justice concerns scales, money, violence, speech, and inherited systems; kindness and humility govern how it is practiced.

## Read the Chapter

Read Micah 6 in the Fluent Translation. Hear God's opening questions as wounded relationship rather than arbitrary accusation.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What history does God recall, what offerings are imagined, and which concrete practices show whether covenant life is present?

### The Chapter in Brief

God summons the mountains as witnesses and asks the people to say how they were wearied. Liberation from Egypt, gifted leaders, and protection from Balak and Balaam are recalled. The worshiper asks whether lavish sacrifices—even a firstborn—could secure acceptance. The answer is already known: do justice, love faithful kindness, and walk humbly with God. The city, however, uses dishonest measures, violence, and lies, continuing corrupt royal traditions and moving toward frustrated labor and disgrace.

### Follow the Story

Grace comes before requirement. God's case begins with rescue and guidance. Covenant response is not payment for salvation but a life shaped by remembered salvation.

The offerings escalate from calves to thousands of animals and rivers of oil, finally imagining child sacrifice. Anxious religion assumes reconciliation must be purchased through excess. Verse 8 rejects that economy. God desires a whole public and relational life, not spectacular harm.

Justice, *hesed*, and humble walking are mutually correcting. Justice without faithful kindness can become self-righteous punishment; kindness without justice can preserve unfair systems; humility keeps both attentive to God rather than moral display.

### Threads Through Scripture

The exodus anchors Israel's ethics throughout the Torah. Samuel and the prophets reject sacrifice used to replace obedience. Jesus joins love of God and neighbor and describes the “weightier matters” as justice, mercy, and faithfulness.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What gifts has the community forgotten before asking what to bring?
- **Understand:** How do the three practices in verse 8 shape one another?
- **Connect:** Which dishonest “weights” have become normal in modern systems?
- **Respond:** What is one just, kind, humble action available this week?

### Prayer and Practice

Choose a routine transaction—pay, pricing, scheduling, credit, or attention—and examine whether its burden falls unfairly. Change one practice.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Balak hired Balaam to curse Israel, but blessing came instead. Omri and Ahab represent an inherited royal pattern of idolatry and injustice rather than isolated personal mistakes.

### Wrestle with This

Why are people often drawn to dramatic religious sacrifice instead of the sustained work of fair relationships?

### Further Study

Read Numbers 22–24, 1 Samuel 15:22–23, and Matthew 23:23.
