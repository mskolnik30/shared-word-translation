# Obadiah 1

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Jerusalem has suffered invasion and plunder. Edom, the neighboring nation associated with Esau, has exploited the disaster of Judah, the people associated with Jacob. Obadiah announces that Edom's apparent security will not protect it from accountability.

### Chapter Path

- **1:1–9:** Proud Edom will be brought down and betrayed by its allies.
- **1:10–14:** Edom's violence against “brother Jacob” is named in an escalating series of actions.
- **1:15–16:** The day of the LORD extends judgment to all nations; deeds return on the doer.
- **1:17–21:** Survivors, exiles, restored land, judgment, and the LORD's kingdom.

### Watch For

Notice the movement from looking to rejoicing, entering, plundering, blocking escape, and handing over survivors. The poem rejects the claim that Edom remained neutral.

## Read the Chapter

Read Obadiah in the Fluent Translation, preferably aloud. Let the poetic images and repeated “day” language shape the movement.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What made Edom feel secure? What actions brought judgment? Where does the poem finally locate kingship?

### The Chapter in Brief

Edom's rocky heights, alliances, wisdom, and military strength cannot preserve it. Its guilt comes from violence against Judah during Jerusalem's calamity: standing aloof, celebrating, plundering, stopping escape, and delivering survivors. The approaching day of the LORD applies a corresponding judgment to Edom and all nations. The poem then promises escape on Zion, restored possession for displaced Israelites, and the final declaration that the kingdom belongs to the LORD.

### Follow the Story

Pride deceives Edom into treating geography as invulnerability. Obadiah's questions about thieves and grape gatherers intensify the reversal: ordinary raiders leave something, but Edom's supposed friends will search it out completely.

The central charge is relational. Edom acted against a brother people. Watching becomes “being like one of” the invaders because Edom's later acts reveal participation.

The final restoration uses territorial and consuming-fire imagery that reflects the trauma and justice language of the poem. Readers should hear the demand for accountability without claiming divine authorization for modern ethnic hatred or retaliatory violence.

### Threads Through Scripture

Genesis tells the difficult relationship of Jacob and Esau, including both conflict and reconciliation. The prophets repeatedly announce a day when violent nations face judgment and the displaced receive a future. The New Testament continues the conviction that vengeance belongs to God rather than to private revenge.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** How does the poem describe the progression from spectatorship to active betrayal?
- **Understand:** Why does kinship intensify rather than lessen Edom's guilt?
- **Connect:** When can neutrality protect the powerful instead of the harmed?
- **Respond:** What concrete action can protect people who are being isolated, exploited, or trapped?

### Prayer and Practice

Pray for people displaced by violence. Learn what one trustworthy local or international organization says is needed, and choose a form of support that does not center you.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

The exact historical crisis behind the poem is debated, though Jerusalem's destruction by Babylon is a common setting. Sepharad cannot be located with certainty. These questions should remain open where the text is open.

### Wrestle with This

Verse 18 says no survivor will remain to the house of Esau. Elsewhere Scripture continues to speak of Edomites after particular judgments. Prophetic totality language communicates decisive reversal; it should be interpreted within the poetic and historical form rather than turned into a timeless command against descendants.

### Further Study

Read Genesis 33 and Psalm 137:7. Compare memories of brotherhood, betrayal, and the desire for justice.
