# 1 Samuel 15

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Saul is Israel's first king. He has already acted impatiently under military pressure, and Samuel has warned that his kingdom will not endure. Chapter 15 narrates a command to place Amalek under total destruction, Saul's selective compliance, and his rejection as king. The command and Samuel's killing of Agag are severe. They must not be generalized into permission for modern ethnic, political, or religious violence.

### Chapter Path

- **15:1–9:** Saul attacks Amalek but spares King Agag and desirable livestock.
- **15:10–16:** God regrets making Saul king; Samuel grieves and confronts Saul.
- **15:17–23:** Saul claims obedience and blames the people; Samuel says listening is better than sacrifice.
- **15:24–31:** Saul confesses while seeking public honor; Samuel announces the kingdom has been torn away.
- **15:32–35:** Samuel executes Agag; Samuel and Saul separate permanently.

### Watch For

Listen for Saul's changing explanations. Notice the contrast between “the LORD your God” and Samuel's speech about Saul's relationship to the LORD. Watch how worship language can become a cover for keeping what is profitable.

## Read the Chapter

Read 1 Samuel 15 in the Fluent Translation. Do not allow the memorable line about obedience and sacrifice to erase the violent setting in which it appears.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does Saul do, how does he explain it, and what does Samuel identify as the central failure?

### The Chapter in Brief

Samuel commissions Saul to strike Amalek and place everything under destruction. Saul defeats them but spares Agag and the best livestock. God tells Samuel of regret over Saul's kingship. Saul greets Samuel with a claim of complete obedience, but the animals expose his words. He blames the people and says the livestock was preserved for sacrifice. Samuel answers that listening matters more than offerings and announces Saul's rejection. After Saul seeks public honor, Samuel executes Agag and leaves Saul.

### Follow the Story

Saul's failure is not simple inactivity. He carries out much of the command while reserving what appears valuable and then renames that selective obedience as worship. His monument to himself and concern about honor before the elders show a ruler managing his public image while resisting truth.

Samuel's statement that obedience is better than sacrifice does not demean worship. It rejects using worship to compensate for refusing God's word. Religious performance cannot make exploitation, concealment, or self-protection faithful.

The chapter says God regrets making Saul king, then says Israel's Glory does not lie or change his mind like a human. The same Hebrew root stands behind the language, creating a real tension. God's grief over Saul is not fickleness, and God's settled verdict is not emotional distance. The narrator lets divine sorrow and faithfulness remain together.

The destruction of Amalek and Samuel's execution of Agag remain morally disturbing. The narrative roots the conflict in remembered Amalekite violence and presents Agag's death as retribution, but readers should not romanticize it. Nothing here authorizes Christians to identify a modern people as Amalek and attack them.

### Threads Through Scripture

Israel first encounters Amalek in Exodus 17; Deuteronomy remembers attacks on those lagging behind. Prophets repeatedly insist that worship without justice and listening is empty. Jesus likewise places obedient love above religious display and rejects violent attempts to secure his kingdom.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** How many times does Saul shift responsibility or seek to preserve honor?
- **Understand:** Why can sacrifice become a religious excuse rather than obedience?
- **Connect:** Where do leaders manage appearances instead of telling the truth about harm?
- **Respond:** Is there a partial obedience you have been renaming as faithfulness?

### Prayer and Practice

Ask God for courage to confess without blaming others or bargaining for reputation. Practice one specific repair that matches your words.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

The Hebrew term *herem* refers to placing people or things under irreversible devotion to destruction. It belongs to a specific ancient war context and is not ordinary warfare policy. Ancient battle narratives also use comprehensive language in ways that raise historical questions, but literary convention does not remove the moral difficulty.

### Wrestle with This

How can readers acknowledge that the chapter attributes a violent command to God while refusing both easy defense and dismissal—and firmly rejecting its use for present violence?

### Further Study

Compare Saul's two confrontations in 1 Samuel 13 and 15, and read Psalm 51 as a later biblical model of confession without self-excuse.
