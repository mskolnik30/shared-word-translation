# 1 Chronicles 21

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

David has received covenant promise, victories, and growing power. He now orders a census over Joab's objection. The chapter never states exactly why counting is wrong; the issue appears bound to royal control, military confidence, and treating people who belong to God as the king's possession.

### Chapter Path

- **21:1–6:** An adversary incites David; Joab objects but carries out most of the census.
- **21:7–13:** David confesses; through Gad, God places three judgments before him.
- **21:14–17:** Plague kills many; God stops the destroying angel, and David accepts responsibility.
- **21:18–30:** David buys Ornan's threshing floor, offers costly sacrifice, and receives fire from heaven.

### Watch For

Notice who bears the cost of a ruler's decision. Also follow the movement of the angel's sword—from threat over Jerusalem to being returned to its sheath.

## Read the Chapter

Read 1 Chronicles 21 in the Fluent Translation. Hold divine mercy and devastating judgment together without making either cancel the other.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does David command, how does he respond after judgment begins, and how does the site of disaster become a place of worship?

### The Chapter in Brief

An adversary incites David to count Israel. Joab objects, but David prevails. After the census, God offers David a choice among famine, military defeat, and plague. David chooses to fall into God's merciful hand. The plague kills seventy thousand before God commands the angel to stop. David identifies himself as the sinner and the people as sheep. At God's direction he purchases Ornan's threshing floor at full price, sacrifices, and receives fire from heaven.

### Follow the Story

The “adversary” initiates the test, but David remains accountable for his command and confession. Joab's protest shows that the danger was recognizable. Counting can be ordinary administration; here it becomes a royal act that converts a people into measurable strength.

David's choice to fall into God's hand appeals to divine mercy, yet the people suffer terribly. His later plea finally sounds like shepherding: he names the people as sheep and asks that judgment fall on his own house. Leadership responsibility becomes more than regret about consequences.

Ornan offers the site and materials freely, but David refuses worship that costs him nothing. The purchase makes repentance tangible. Chronicles will identify this place with the future temple site, so remembered failure becomes part of Israel's worship rather than being erased from institutional memory.

### Threads Through Scripture

The chapter retells 2 Samuel 24 with important differences. Its shepherd language recalls the Bible's expectations for kings. Costly repentance anticipates prophetic insistence that worship must include justice and repair.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** When does David begin speaking as one responsible for the people rather than over them?
- **Understand:** Why might a census express control rather than neutral knowledge here?
- **Connect:** Who bears hidden costs when leaders pursue impressive measurements?
- **Respond:** What would make one confession materially costly and restorative?

### Prayer and Practice

Review one number you use to judge success. Ask whose life it represents and what it conceals. Choose one act of repair that costs more than words.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Chronicles names “Satan,” meaning adversary or accuser, while Samuel says the LORD's anger incites David. Read the accounts together without forcing a quick harmonization. The six hundred shekels likely purchase the larger site, not only the immediate threshing floor.

### Wrestle with This

How do you speak honestly about mercy in a story where many people die because of a king's act?

### Further Study

Compare 2 Samuel 24 and 1 Chronicles 22:1 to see how the retelling links the altar with temple preparation.
