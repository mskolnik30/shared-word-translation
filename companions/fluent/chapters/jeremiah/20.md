# Jeremiah 20

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Jeremiah has smashed a jar as a sign of Jerusalem's coming destruction. Pashhur, a temple official, responds with violence and public restraint. The chapter then opens Jeremiah's inner life: accusation, compulsion, fear, confidence, praise, and a wish never to have been born.

### Chapter Path

- **20:1–6:** Pashhur strikes and confines Jeremiah; the prophet renames him “Terror on Every Side.”
- **20:7–10:** Jeremiah says the calling has overpowered him; God's word is ridicule outside and fire within.
- **20:11–13:** Confidence and praise interrupt the complaint.
- **20:14–18:** Jeremiah curses the day of his birth and ends with unresolved sorrow.

### Watch For

Do not force the chapter into a smooth emotional recovery. Praise is real, and despair returns afterward. Scripture preserves both.

## Read the Chapter

Read Jeremiah 20 in the Fluent Translation. If the closing wish not to have been born touches your own despair, pause and contact someone safe; the poem gives pain a voice but does not require you to carry it alone.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How do institutional punishment, the burden of speech, trust, and despair coexist in Jeremiah's experience?

### The Chapter in Brief

Pashhur beats Jeremiah and places him in stocks. Released the next day, Jeremiah announces judgment and exile. Jeremiah then complains that God's call has made him a public joke. When he tries not to speak, the word burns like fire inside him. He hears threats but also confesses that God is with him against persecutors and briefly sings praise. The chapter ends by cursing his birth and asking why his life contains toil, sorrow, and shame.

### Follow the Story

Opposition comes from temple leadership, showing that religious office can defend false security against a painful true word. Pashhur's new name turns the terror he helped silence into the reality he will face.

Jeremiah describes his call with language of being enticed and overpowered. This is not serene vocational fulfillment. He cannot escape the word, yet speaking isolates and endangers him. The fire within is both compulsion and pain.

The final lament matters because it follows praise. Faith is not a straight ascent from distress to confidence. Jeremiah's agony remains inside his prophetic vocation and inside Scripture. If a reader shares his wish not to have been born, care should include immediate human support and qualified help, not only exhortation.

### Threads Through Scripture

Job also curses his birth, while lament psalms move unpredictably between protest and trust. Jesus is mocked and opposed by religious authorities, and Paul describes being pressed without pretending pain is unreal.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What changes—and what does not—after Jeremiah praises?
- **Understand:** Why is false prophecy a communal danger rather than a private mistake?
- **Connect:** How can religious institutions punish speech that threatens their security?
- **Respond:** Who can help you carry a calling that has become isolating?

### Prayer and Practice

Tell God the truth without manufacturing resolution. Check in directly with a person carrying public pressure or despair, and help them access sustained support.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

The stocks were an instrument of restraint and humiliation. “Kidneys and heart” names hidden motives and desires. Jeremiah asks God to judge those motives rather than taking private vengeance.

### Wrestle with This

What changes when despairing speech is received as part of relationship with God rather than automatic failure of faith?

### Further Study

Read Jeremiah 1:4–10, Job 3, Psalm 88, and 2 Corinthians 4:7–12.
