# 1 John 3

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

The writer has urged believers to remain in Christ as children of God. Chapter 3 explores that identity: God's love names them as children now, future transformation gives hope, and family likeness becomes visible in righteousness and love.

### Chapter Path

- **3:1–3:** God's love, present identity, future likeness, and purifying hope.
- **3:4–10:** Sin, righteousness, and the revealed work of the Son.
- **3:11–18:** Love contrasted with Cain's hatred; Jesus' self-giving becomes the pattern for material care.
- **3:19–24:** Reassurance when the heart condemns; confidence, prayer, trust, love, and the Spirit.

### Watch For

Hold the chapter beside 1:8–2:2. The writer does not suddenly claim that believers never fail. He describes the incompatible directions of a life ruled by sin and a life born from God.

## Read the Chapter

Read 1 John 3 in the Fluent Translation. Watch how identity, action, and assurance relate without becoming identical.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What are believers now, and what do they await? How does Jesus define love? What reassures a condemning heart?

### The Chapter in Brief

God's remarkable love has made believers children of God, though their future likeness to Christ is not yet fully visible. The Son came to take away sin and destroy the devil's works, so a life born from God cannot make sin its settled practice. The message from the beginning is to love one another, not follow Cain's murderous resentment. Jesus lays down his life, showing what love is. Those with resources cannot close their compassion to a sibling in need. Love acts in truth. When the heart condemns, God is greater than the heart, and the Spirit confirms God's remaining presence.

### Follow the Story

The chapter's moral seriousness grows from gift: “See what kind of love the Father has given us.” Children do not earn their identity through flawless behavior. They are called to resemble the family into which grace has brought them.

Love is then made concrete. Willingness to lay down life cannot be separated from willingness to open one's resources. Dramatic declarations that ignore ordinary need fail the chapter's test.

### Threads Through Scripture

Cain's murder of Abel gives the chapter its negative pattern: resentment that refuses a sibling's life. Jesus provides the positive pattern through self-giving love. The command to love God and neighbor converges in a community that protects life and meets need.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What is already true of God's children, and what is not yet revealed?
- **Understand:** How do 1:8–2:2 help interpret the strong statements about sin here?
- **Connect:** Where is it easier to speak of sacrificial love than to share resources?
- **Respond:** What need can you meet in a way that preserves the recipient's dignity?

### Prayer and Practice

Choose one practical act of love with a name, time, and cost. Do it without using generosity to control the other person. If your heart condemns you vaguely, ask what is actually yours to confess and what belongs to fear rather than truth.

## Go Deeper

### How to Read This

The verbs about sin can describe continuing or characteristic action. Christian interpreters explain the grammar in different ways, but the letter itself requires two truths to remain together: believers still need confession and advocacy, and new birth is incompatible with surrendering one's life to sin.

### Wrestle with This

“We ought to lay down our lives” must never be used to demand that someone endure abuse or erase healthy boundaries. Jesus' self-giving seeks life and defeats destructive power. Love can require distance, reporting harm, or protecting vulnerable people.

### Further Study

Compare 1 John 3:16–18 with Deuteronomy 15:7–11 and James 2:14–17. What makes compassion visible?
