# Proverbs 31

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Proverbs closes with two teachings. An unnamed mother instructs King Lemuel about disciplined power and advocacy. Then an alphabetic poem praises a “woman of strength” whose wisdom appears in enterprise, planning, generosity, speech, household care, and reverence for God. The poem is praise, not a performance checklist imposed on every woman.

### Chapter Path

- **31:1–9:** A royal mother warns against destructive sex and impaired judgment and commands defense of vulnerable people.
- **31:10–19:** A woman of strength earns trust, secures materials, trades, plans, purchases land, and works vigorously.
- **31:20–27:** Her strength includes generosity, preparedness, dignity, wise speech, and household oversight.
- **31:28–31:** Her family praises her; works rooted in fear of the LORD speak publicly on her behalf.

### Watch For

Notice how the chapter frames strength: royal power is disciplined for justice, and household strength creates flourishing shared with poor people.

## Read the Chapter

Read Proverbs 31 in the Fluent Translation. Let the repeated active verbs define the woman's strength more than later stereotypes do.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What does Lemuel's mother expect from a king? What forms of capability, care, and public contribution does the poem praise?

### The Chapter in Brief

King Lemuel receives teaching from his mother: do not surrender royal strength to destructive patterns or cloud judgment when afflicted people need justice. Speak for those who cannot secure a hearing and defend poor and needy people. The chapter then praises a capable woman. She manages trade and household provision, buys and develops a field, works with strength, helps the poor, prepares for the future, teaches steadfast love, and is praised by family and community. Reverence for the LORD, not charm or beauty, grounds enduring praise.

### Follow the Story

The king's mother is the chapter's first wisdom teacher. Her warning about women addresses a ruler's misuse of sexual power, not the idea that women are dangerous by nature. Her concern about alcohol is likewise vocational: a ruler must remember law and the claims of afflicted people.

“Woman of strength” can also mean valor, capacity, or excellence. Ruth receives the same description. This woman's work moves across household, marketplace, agriculture, textiles, property, teaching, charity, and civic reputation. The portrait does not confine her to passive domesticity.

The Hebrew poem is an acrostic, each verse advancing through the alphabet. Its comprehensive shape creates memorable praise. It should not become a daily scorecard used to exhaust, compare, or shame women. Wisdom literature offers a portrait worth celebrating, not a claim that one person must perform every task alone.

### Threads Through Scripture

Woman Wisdom in Proverbs 1–9 calls publicly, feeds learners, and builds a house. Ruth embodies strong loyal action, and Proverbs 31's concern for those without a voice echoes the Torah and prophets. Jesus describes greatness as using power to serve rather than dominate.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which actions combine economic ability with care for others?
- **Understand:** Why does the chapter begin with instruction to a king?
- **Connect:** When does praise become an unfair expectation?
- **Respond:** Whose overlooked labor or wisdom can you name publicly?

### Prayer and Practice

Thank someone specifically for work that helps others flourish. Use whatever influence you have to amplify a person who struggles to receive a fair hearing.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

The woman's household is economically substantial and includes servants, so the poem does not describe every household's resources. Praise at the city gates places her work within public civic recognition.

### Wrestle with This

How can communities receive this poem as liberation into capable wisdom rather than pressure to perform an impossible ideal?

### Further Study

Compare Ruth 3:11, Proverbs 8–9, and Proverbs 14:1; trace how wisdom, strength, household, and public good connect.
