# Colossians 3

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Paul has said fullness dwells in Christ and warned against regulations that appear wise but cannot transform desire. Chapter 3 describes raised life through practices the community must put to death, put away, and put on.

### Chapter Path

- **3:1–4:** Seek what is above, where Christ reigns; believers' life is hidden with him.
- **3:5–11:** Put to death exploitative desires and put away anger, slander, and lies; the new humanity is renewed across social divisions.
- **3:12–17:** Put on compassion, humility, patience, forgiveness, love, peace, gratitude, shared teaching, and worship.
- **3:18–25:** Wives, husbands, children, parents, and enslaved people are addressed under the Lord's impartial judgment.

### Watch For

“Above” is defined by the enthroned Christ and leads into earthly responsibility. It is not contempt for creation, work, bodies, or relationships.

## Read the Chapter

Read Colossians 3 in the Fluent Translation. Follow the clothing verbs and the repeated “one another.”

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

What belongs to the old humanity, what clothes the new, and how does the Lord relativize household power?

### The Chapter in Brief

Those raised with Christ seek his reign and put destructive desire, greed, anger, abuse, and lying to death. They have put off the old humanity and put on the new, where ethnic, cultural, and status distinctions no longer establish unequal worth because Christ is all and in all. God's chosen people clothe themselves with compassion, humility, patience, forgiveness, and love. Christ's peace governs the shared heart and word, song, gratitude, and action. Household members are addressed, and every wrongdoer is warned that the Lord shows no partiality.

### Follow the Story

The new-humanity image is corporate as well as personal. Paul's ancient labels include groups stereotyped as uncivilized; placing them inside “Christ all and in all” rejects ranking without pretending social prejudice has disappeared.

Teaching is reciprocal: the word dwells richly as believers teach and warn one another. Peace “rules” like an arbiter in one body, deciding conflicts toward shared wholeness.

The household section assumes patriarchy and slavery rather than abolishing them. It places constraints on power—husbands must not be harsh, fathers must not crush courage, and wrongdoers face impartial judgment—but does not provide a sufficient modern household ethic. Enslaved people are not employees, and the passage must never justify owning people or keeping someone in abuse.

### Threads Through Scripture

The creation image recalls Genesis; baptismal unity parallels Galatians 3. Jesus places greatness in service, and Philemon pressures a slaveholding household to receive Onesimus as a beloved brother.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which practices are individual, and which require “one another”?
- **Understand:** How does Christ's peace act like an arbiter?
- **Connect:** Which old status labels still determine whose voice matters?
- **Respond:** What communal garment needs deliberate practice?

### Prayer and Practice

Choose one “put off” and one “put on” for a real relationship. Make the action observable rather than merely inward.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Ancient households were economic systems headed by free men and including wives, children, dependents, and slaves. “In the Lord” contests absolute household authority but does not erase the structure in this text.

### Wrestle with This

How can churches honor the chapter's transformed relationships while refusing to preserve the domination its ancient household still assumes?

### Further Study

Read Galatians 3:26–29, Ephesians 4:20–32, and Philemon.
