# Ephesians 5

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Grace has created one reconciled body, and chapter 4 called it to mature unity, truthful speech, honest labor, generosity, and forgiveness. Chapter 5 continues with three ways of walking—love, light, and wisdom—before entering household relationships through mutual submission.

### Chapter Path

- **5:1–7:** Imitate God through Christ-shaped self-giving love; refuse exploitative sexuality, greed, and degrading speech.
- **5:8–14:** Those who were darkness are now light in the Lord and should expose fruitless works.
- **5:15–21:** Walk wisely, make good use of dangerous time, be filled with the Spirit, and submit to one another.
- **5:22–33:** Wives and husbands are addressed through the mystery of Christ and church; husbands are commanded to love self-givingly.

### Watch For

Mutual submission in verse 21 grammatically opens the household section. The command to husbands is not to rule but to love after Christ's giving of himself.

## Read the Chapter

Read Ephesians 5 in the Fluent Translation. Notice that Spirit filling becomes singing, thanksgiving, and mutual submission—not individual display.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How are love, light, wisdom, Spirit, and household relationships connected?

### The Chapter in Brief

Believers imitate God by walking in Christ's sacrificial love, rejecting exploitation and greed. Because they are light in the Lord, their lives should bear goodness, righteousness, and truth. Wisdom makes the most of the time, rejects drunkenness, and receives continual Spirit filling through shared worship, gratitude, and mutual submission. Wives are addressed in relation to husbands; husbands must love as Christ loved and gave himself for the church, nourishing rather than harming. Marriage becomes a mystery pointing toward Christ and church.

### Follow the Story

Greed is idolatry because acquisitive desire treats more as a rival god. Love is not sentiment but costly action for another's good.

Household instructions speak within a patriarchal world, but the paragraph's controlling movement is mutual submission and Christ's self-giving. “Head” remains a debated metaphor and should not be casually replaced with “boss.” Nothing here authorizes coercive control or abuse. A spouse facing danger may seek safety; submission does not give another person ownership of body, conscience, or access.

The oldest Greek in verse 22 depends on the verb from verse 21, so separating the paragraphs too sharply hides the connection. Husbands receive the longer and more disruptive command: surrender self-protective power for nourishing love.

### Threads Through Scripture

The one-flesh quotation comes from Genesis 2. Jesus gives himself for others and rejects domination as leadership. Colossians offers a shorter parallel, while Revelation pictures the church as bride through different imagery.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** What communal actions follow “be filled with the Spirit”?
- **Understand:** How does Christ define love and authority?
- **Connect:** How have household texts been used either to preserve or challenge domination?
- **Respond:** What mutual practice would make a close relationship safer and more life-giving?

### Prayer and Practice

Ask where your power can become nourishment rather than control. If there is abuse, prioritize safety and qualified support rather than forced mutuality.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Roman household codes commonly addressed male household heads. Ephesians also addresses people with less public power directly, recognizing them as moral hearers while transforming obligations “in the Lord.”

### Wrestle with This

What does mutual submission require from the person who holds more social power?

### Further Study

Read Genesis 2:18–25, Mark 10:35–45, Colossians 3:12–25, and Ephesians 4:25–32.
