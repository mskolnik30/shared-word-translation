# Song of Songs 2

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

The Song has opened with mutual desire and praise. Chapter 2 moves from comparisons of flower and tree, to an embrace, to a repeated warning about love's timing, and then to a springtime invitation. The speakers are identified from context; ancient manuscripts do not label every voice.

### Chapter Path

- **2:1–6:** The lovers compare one another to flower and fruit tree; desire includes delight, nourishment, and embrace.
- **2:7:** The woman asks the daughters of Jerusalem not to awaken love before it pleases.
- **2:8–14:** The beloved arrives like a gazelle and invites her into the renewal of spring.
- **2:15–17:** Little foxes threaten blossoming vineyards; the woman affirms mutual belonging and continuing longing.

### Watch For

Notice how desire and patience belong together. The poem celebrates embodied attraction without treating intimacy as something one person may demand from another.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

How do the speakers describe delight, invitation, timing, threats, and mutual belonging?

### The Chapter in Brief

The woman and her beloved praise one another through images of flowers and trees. She delights in his shade, fruit, public love, and embrace, then warns that love should not be awakened before its proper time. She hears him approaching and remembers his invitation to rise and come away because winter has passed and the land is alive. Small foxes threaten the blossoming vineyards. The chapter ends with mutual belonging and longing for the beloved's return.

### Follow the Story

These poems let both lovers speak. The woman names her own desire, bodily experience, delight, and boundaries. Her voice is not a passive response to male initiative; it carries much of the chapter's movement.

Spring gathers sensory abundance—flowers, song, figs, vines, fragrance—and makes invitation feel like emergence from confinement. The beloved asks to see her face and hear her voice. Desire is attentive to the other person's presence and speech.

The refrain about not awakening love “until it pleases” resists hurry and control. Love has power, but power does not create entitlement. Read within the Song, the refrain supports patience, mutuality, and timing rather than shame about desire.

The little foxes probably picture small threats that can damage something beginning to flourish. The poem does not identify them, leaving readers to consider how neglect, secrecy, contempt, pressure, or divided attention can injure trust.

### Threads Through Scripture

Gardens and fruitful land recall creation's goodness and later prophetic pictures of renewal. Biblical covenant language sometimes uses marriage imagery for God and the people, which helped Jewish and Christian readers hear further meanings. Those readings can enrich the poem when they do not erase its celebration of human love.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which senses—sight, sound, taste, touch, smell—shape the poem?
- **Understand:** How does the refrain place restraint beside desire?
- **Connect:** What “little foxes” can damage trust before people notice the loss?
- **Respond:** How can you honor another person's voice and timing in any close relationship?

### Prayer and Practice

Give thanks for embodied life, beauty, and affection without demanding them. Practice one act of attentive consent: ask, listen, and receive the answer without pressure.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

“House of wine” suggests celebration and abundance. The “banner” communicates visible belonging. The exact flowers and fruits are less certain than their shared imagery of beauty and nourishment.

### Wrestle with This

How can communities teach that desire is good while also forming patience, mutuality, safety, and freedom?

### Further Study

Trace the repeated refrain in Song of Songs 2:7, 3:5, and 8:4, then read the claim about love's strength in 8:6–7.
