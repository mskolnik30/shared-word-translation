# Ezekiel 16

## Before You Read

### Where You Are

Ezekiel is among exiles while Jerusalem still stands. Chapter 16 is a long allegory containing exposed infancy, marriage, sexualized accusation, child sacrifice, threatened public stripping and violence, comparison with Samaria and Sodom, and unexpected covenant restoration. Its rhetoric must never be used to shame women, survivors, or sex workers.

### Chapter Path

- **16:1–14:** Jerusalem is pictured as an abandoned infant whom God rescues, raises, covenants with, and adorns.
- **16:15–34:** Jerusalem uses received gifts for idols, alliances, and child sacrifice.
- **16:35–52:** Judgment is described through violent exposure; Jerusalem is compared unfavorably with Samaria and Sodom.
- **16:53–63:** Restoration reaches the sisters too; God remembers covenant and establishes an everlasting covenant.

### Watch For

The controlling metaphor shifts from rescue to marriage and betrayal. Keep the political and religious referents in view; do not translate metaphor into claims about the moral character of women.

## Read the Chapter

Read Ezekiel 16 in the Fluent Translation only if and as you are able. Skipping or pausing violent imagery is permitted.

## After You Read

### Tell It in Your Own Words

Without repeating degrading details, trace rescue, gift, betrayal, judgment, comparison, and renewed covenant.

### The Chapter in Brief

Jerusalem is portrayed as an unwanted newborn left in blood. God commands her to live, raises her, enters covenant, and gives beauty and abundance. She trusts that beauty, redirects gifts toward idols and foreign alliances, and sacrifices children. The allegory announces humiliating judgment and says Jerusalem has made Sodom and Samaria appear less guilty. Yet God will restore all three and remember the covenant, establishing an everlasting covenant and making atonement.

### Follow the Story

The opening “Live in your blood” centers rescue from exposure and death. Every later gift—clothing, food, jewelry, status—is received before it is misused. The accusation is therefore about covenant betrayal and political dependence, not sexual desire by itself.

The chapter's punishment imagery reproduces sexual humiliation. Ancient prophetic rhetoric uses the violated female body to picture a city's shame. Readers can understand the metaphor's force while also naming its danger, especially for people whose bodies have been shamed or assaulted. Divine judgment here cannot authorize human sexual violence.

Verse 49 refuses a narrow account of Sodom: pride, abundance, ease, and failure to strengthen poor and needy people are explicitly named. The closing restoration of Sodom and Samaria is startling. Judgment does not end God's action, and Jerusalem cannot imagine mercy as private superiority.

### Threads Through Scripture

The rescued infant echoes Israel's powerless beginnings; covenant marriage imagery appears in Hosea and Jeremiah. The everlasting covenant and divine atonement anticipate Ezekiel's promises of a new heart, Spirit, and restored presence.

### Questions for Conversation

- **Notice:** Which gifts are received before they are misused?
- **Understand:** What political and religious realities does the sexual metaphor carry?
- **Connect:** How can teachers explain harmful imagery without repeating its harm?
- **Respond:** Where can abundance be redirected toward strengthening poor people?

### Prayer and Practice

Refuse body-shaming language in moral or religious speech. Support a survivor-centered or poverty-relief practice that restores dignity.

## Go Deeper

### What Needs Context

Jerusalem's Amorite and Hittite “parents” are rhetorical, not literal ancestry. Spreading a wing is protective and marital imagery. “Everlasting covenant” shifts the ending beyond deserved judgment toward God's action.

### Wrestle with This

How can readers receive the chapter's indictment of exploitation while refusing its sexualized humiliation as a model for speech?

### Further Study

Read Ezekiel 18, 34, and 36:22–32 alongside Hosea 2 and Luke 4:16–21.
