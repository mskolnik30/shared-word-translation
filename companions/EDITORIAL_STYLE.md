# Companion Editorial Style

## Inclusive, non-exhaustive identity language

Every person is within the scope of claims about human dignity, safety, belonging, and divine regard. A list of communities or identity dimensions supplies examples; it never defines the boundary of that claim.

When identity language is materially relevant, use this sequence:

1. **State the universal claim first.** Say who is protected or included without making the reader infer it from a list.
2. **Mark examples as non-exhaustive.** Use language such as “including,” “among the many,” “such as,” or “not limited to.”
3. **Name communities that the passage or its reception has materially placed at risk.** Do not hide a relevant community inside a vague catch-all. Sexual orientation and gender identity or expression should be named when LGBTQIA+ people or interpretations are materially implicated.
4. **Close universally.** Reaffirm that no unnamed identity, status, body, history, or circumstance falls outside the claim.

Do not use “etc.” for people. Avoid a bare series of identity categories after words such as “by” or “regardless of”; readers can reasonably experience such a list as complete. A phrase such as “and others” is not a substitute for the universal claim, but it may reinforce one.

This standard does not require every paragraph to name every community. It requires every list to say, in substance and structure, that the list is not the limit. Specificity and universality belong together.

### Preferred pattern

> No person may be denied dignity or safety. Race, ethnicity, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, disability, nationality, citizenship, wealth, social standing, and religious identity are among the many differences that have been used to rank human worth; none places a person outside this protection.

Adapt the examples to the passage and the documented history of interpretation. Preserve the universal opening and close.
